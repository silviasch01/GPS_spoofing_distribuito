# Theoretical limits of relative-geometry GNSS spoofing detectors under a coordinated rigid translation (null-space / unobservability notes, incl. GNSS/INS and SemperFi)

Scope: civilian GNSS, 2011–2026. Notation used throughout: receivers i = 1..n with true positions x_i(t); spoofed fixes z_i(t) = x_i(t) + b_i(t) + n_i(t); a *rigid (common-mode) translation* is b_i(t) = b(t) for all i. Fields I could not verify from a primary/bibliographic source are marked "unverified".

Primary sources were downloaded and read in full text where indicated ("full text read"); others were checked only through abstracts/metadata (Crossref, publisher pages).

## KQ1. Coordinated / multi-transmitter / distributed spoofing that imposes a common rigid translation on a formation: what the literature says, and whether it is acknowledged as undetectable by relative-consistency checks

### Takeaway
The literature acknowledges — first implicitly (Tippenhauer et al., CCS 2011: a formation-preserving group-spoof always has a solution, namely a rigid transformation of the satellite geometry), then explicitly (GNSS-WASP, USENIX Security 2025: a multi-transmitter attack that "preserves their true formation (i.e., their relative distances)" and "bypasses state-of-the-art spoofing countermeasures that rely on constellations of receivers with known distances and those that rely on sudden, unpredictable movements"; Park & Yoo, arXiv 2026: "a common-mode b(t) is unobservable from relative channels alone", framed as gauge freedom from network-localization theory) — that a common rigid translation lies in the null space of every detector that is a function of relative quantities only. The 2011–2024 multi-receiver detector papers (Tippenhauer 2011 §6, Jansen 2016) explicitly assume a single-antenna or location-constrained attacker and treat the multi-antenna formation-preserving attacker as "very difficult", not impossible.

### Cited Findings

**Tippenhauer, Pöpper, Rasmussen, Čapkun — CCS 2011 (full text read)**
- Full citation: Nils Ole Tippenhauer, Christina Pöpper, Kasper Bonne Rasmussen, Srdjan Čapkun, "On the Requirements for Successful GPS Spoofing Attacks," *Proceedings of the 18th ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security (CCS '11)*, Chicago, 2011, pp. 75–86, DOI 10.1145/2046707.2046719 — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/2046707.2046719); open-access PDF: [cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf). (Some secondary listings give pp. 75–85; Crossref/ACM give 75–86.)
- Result 1 (§4.2): "One or more receivers V_j ∈ V can be spoofed to any one location L′ using a single attacker antenna. Spoofing multiple receivers to the same location L′ will generally lead to different time offsets δ′_j at each victim." — [Tippenhauer 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- §4.3: "if the attacker is using only one transmission antenna, any possible placement of this antenna will lead to two victims computing their location to the same coordinates L′ … Hence, the attacker cannot use only one antenna to shift the victims to different locations. We will now show that, using multiple antennas, the attacker can spoof two victims to any locations while preserving their mutual time offsets". The paper then "focus[es] on attacks in which multiple victims are shifted to a set of new locations that preserve their mutual distances and mutual time offsets." — [Tippenhauer 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Result 3 (necessary condition): for all victims V_j, V_k and all signals s_i, b′_ijk ≤ |P_j − P_k| where b′_ijk = R′_ij − R′_ik = |L′_j − L^A_i| − |L′_k − L^A_i| + Δ′_j − Δ′_k is the pseudorange difference of the i-th (impersonated) satellite between two spoofed locations. — [Tippenhauer 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Results 4–6: with 3, 4, ≥5 victims spoofed to arbitrary distinct locations, the admissible placements of the attacker antenna impersonating satellite i are, respectively, the intersection curve of two hyperboloids, at most two points, and at most one point. — [Tippenhauer 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- **Result 7 (the key statement for a rigid transformation):** "When spoofing a group of GPS receivers V_1,…,V_n such that the formation (i.e., the mutual distances and relative time offsets) is preserved, there is always at least one solution to the decisional group GPS spoofing problem." Proof sketch in the paper: "If the formation of the victims is preserved, there exists a bijective affine augmented transformation matrix T which describes this translation and rotation … T·L_j = L′_j. Then, the inverse transformation T^{-1} applied to L^A_i will yield a possible antenna placement P^A_i = T^{-1}·L^A_i, because all pseudoranges R′_ij between L′_j and L^A_i and the measured range R^A_ij between P^A_i and P_j will be the same (the transformation preserves the Euclidean distance)." And: "As a consequence of Results 6 and 7, spoofing five or more receivers while retaining their formation has exactly one solution, an affine transformation of the claimed satellite position L^A_i." — [Tippenhauer 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- §6 (the multi-receiver countermeasure) explicitly bounds its own scope: "Even if only two GPS receivers are used, this countermeasure can detect any attacker that is only using a single antenna … As shown in Results 4–6, a strong attacker using multiple antennas could attempt to send signals such that the mutual distances between multiple receivers are preserved. Nevertheless, each additional receiver of the victim makes these spoofing attacks exceedingly more difficult because the space of possible antenna placements for the attacker gets reduced significantly … From Results 6 and 7 we know that there exists only one location per satellite where the attacker can place his antenna; this location is the rotated and translated satellite position of the GPS signal. Conducting such an attack is very difficult. It becomes even impossible if the victim can hide the exact positioning of at least one GPS receiver from the attacker". Figure 10 caption: "For an attacker with a single antenna, the two-receiver countermeasure is enough. If the attacker uses multiple antennas, four (or more) receivers severely restrict the attacker's antenna placements. Wrong antenna placements will change the distances of the receivers and can thus be detected." — [Tippenhauer 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Note the number-of-antennas statement the assignment quotes ("to spoof n receivers to n chosen positions the attacker needs n antennas") does **not** appear in that form in the paper; the paper's result is per impersonated satellite: one attacker antenna per impersonated satellite, placed (for ≥5 formation-preserving victims) at the unique point T^{-1}·L^A_i. For a one-location spoof a single antenna suffices for any n (Result 1). — [Tippenhauer 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)

- Full citation: Minseok Park, Joon Soo Yoo (Jeonbuk National University), "Rigid-Covert GNSS Spoofing of UAV Swarms: A Structural Blind Spot, Its Detection Limit, and Absolute-Anchor Defenses," arXiv:2608.06885v1 [cs.CR], 7 Aug 2026. Abstract: a common slow translation "preserves all pairwise distances and is therefore unobservable to any relative-only detector." The authors state "the propositions themselves are not new theorems" but instances of network-localization theory (they cite Aspnes et al. 2006). — [arXiv 2608.06885](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06885v1)
- Detectors they classify as blind: distance verification (Mykytyn et al. 2023), Byzantine distance consensus (Zhong et al., *Reliability Engineering & System Safety* 269, 2026, 112101), SDP feasibility (Bi et al. 2024), consensus relocalization (Dev et al. 2025), formation localization via RSS+GNSS/IMU (Michieletto et al. 2023, "likely"), multi-receiver checks (Jansen et al. 2016), and the geometry channel of multimodal detectors (Tariq & Shaukat 2026; Tariq, Ahanger & Shaukat, *Scientific Reports* 16, 2026, 15802). — [arXiv 2608.06885](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06885v1)
- Detection floor for an *anchor-residual* detector (Eq. 1): v*_ideal = 2γ / (1 − t_s/T), with γ the worst-case anchor drift rate, t_s the attack onset, T the horizon, v* the minimum detectable covert ramp rate; operational version adds a noise term v_noise(σ_a, T, α). — [arXiv 2608.06885](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06885v1)
- On GNSS-WASP's countermeasures: they "key on the residual an imperfect wide-area spoof leaves … so they do not address the idealized perfectly common translation we study." — [arXiv 2608.06885](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06885v1)
- Caveat stated by the authors: "All evaluations are simulation-based and use no RF spoofing hardware or physical swarm." — [arXiv 2608.06885](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06885v1)

### Inferences
- **Formal statement for the thesis (synthesising Tippenhauer Result 7, GNSS-WASP §5, Park & Yoo Prop. 1, Aspnes 2006).** Let D be any detector statistic that depends on the fixes only through differences, D = f({z_i − z_j}_{i,j}, {d_ij}) where d_ij are physically measured inter-receiver ranges (UWB, RSS, vision) or known-formation constants. Under b_i(t) = b(t): z_i − z_j = (x_i − x_j) + (n_i − n_j), independent of b. Hence D is identical in distribution under H0 and under the rigid-translation attack, so P_D = P_FA for every threshold — the ROC is the diagonal. The same holds for any rigid motion (translation + rotation) if only distances are used, and for pure translation if headings/baselines are also compared (multi-antenna attitude check). This is exactly the gauge freedom of anchor-free network localization.
- Tippenhauer's Result 7 is the *constructive existence* half: the formation-preserving spoof is realizable by placing one transmitter per impersonated satellite at P^A_i = T^{-1} L^A_i (rigidly moved satellite). GNSS-WASP is the *practical approximation* (transmitters "in line with the satellites at lower altitudes", ≥4 synchronized transmitters, ~1 m residual error at 1 km). The defensive papers of 2011–2016 never claimed to catch this case; they scoped it out as "very difficult"/"impractical".
- The only handle a relative detector keeps is the *imperfection* of the common-mode approximation (GNSS-WASP's bias, which decays with transmitter altitude). For an idealised perfectly common Δ, GNSS-WASP's own authors say detection needs "another trusted positioning source" (see KQ4).
- Note for the thesis: the Park & Yoo 2026 preprint makes almost exactly the claim the chapter wants to make ("structural blind spot", "absolute-anchor defenses"). It should be cited and the chapter's novelty positioned relative to it (e.g., formal null-space framing of GNSS/INS detectors, SemperFi analysis, or experimental RF validation, which that preprint lacks).

## KQ2. SemperFi (GNSS/INS sensor-fusion family): precise description of the Adversarial Peak Identifier and why a constant offset Δ is in its blind spot

### Takeaway
SemperFi's Adversarial Peak Identifier (API in the arXiv text; "APE" in the assignment) compares the *shape of a 10–20 s pseudorandom manoeuvre* as reconstructed by IMU dead-reckoning (EKF decoupled from GPS) against the GPS-logged track, using the average Euclidean deviation with a 5 m threshold; the test is a function of displacements, so a constant offset Δ added to every GPS fix cancels exactly, whereas a time-varying Δ(t) does not. The paper itself states the test is defeated by an attacker whose signals "reflect UAV's random maneuver"; a rigid translation satisfies that condition trivially because it adds nothing to the manoeuvre.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation (arXiv version read in full): Harshad Sathaye, Gerald LaMountain, Pau Closas, Aanjhan Ranganathan, "SemperFi: A Spoofer Eliminating GPS Receiver for UAVs," arXiv:2105.01860 [cs.CR], 5 May 2021, DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2105.01860 — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.01860), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01860). The peer-reviewed version is cited by GNSS-WASP (ref. [52]) as "SemperFi: Anti-spoofing GPS Receiver for UAVs," NDSS 2022; NDSS DOI 10.14722/ndss.2022.23184 as given in the assignment — unverified by me. — [GNSS-WASP PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity25/sec25cycle1-prepub-757-tibaldo.pdf)
- Architecture: three modules, "i) Spoofing Detection, ii) the Adversarial Peak Identifier (API), and finally, iii) Legitimate Signal Retriever (LSR)". Spoofing detection is "based on multiple peaks in the acquisition plot" (auxiliary-peak detector from prior work); API is activated only after detection ("API will trigger the maneuver when the peak separation is more than 500 ns"); LSR performs successive interference cancellation S_L = S_R − S′_AT. — [arXiv 2105.01860 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01860)
- API procedure (verbatim, §3.3): "Once spoofing is detected, API signals the UAV to stop and hold the current position. As the UAV stops, the spoofed signals' location should also reflect this stop in an ideal attack scenario. This is possible as the attacker knows how the UAV is supposed to move based on spoofed trajectories. Once the UAV stops, the UAV performs a specific maneuver consisting of a pseudorandom sequence of turns. The attacker is not aware of the exact maneuver and cannot generate GPS signals that reflect this maneuver. Before performing the maneuver, UAV de-couples GPS and extended Kalman filter (EKF) and uses IMU sensors based dead-reckoning to track the movements. The UAV logs GPS coordinates, but it does not use it for rectifying EKF estimates. API compares the track estimated by IMU sensors and GPS by averaging the Euclidean distance between each deviation sample obtained from IMU sensors and the GPS receiver. Since the attacker cannot generate the signals corresponding to the maneuver, a comparison of track estimates by IMU sensors and GPS shows significant deviations. … On the contrary, if the comparison does not show deviations, it is concluded that the receiver is tracking the legitimate peak even if there is an ongoing spoofing attack." — [arXiv 2105.01860 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01860)
- Threshold and duration (§5.2): "For a commercial navigation grade MEMS sensor, these errors or 'random walks', can be up to 1.59 km/hr [47]. Thus for a 60 s maneuver, position estimates can drift up to 26 m. Based on these characteristics, the API uses a threshold of 5 m to decide whether the receiver is tracking legitimate signals or adversarial signals." "We tested maneuvers with flight time of 10 s to 60 s … increasing the time duration will: i) cause more inaccuracies because of IMU drifts and ii) allows the attacker an increased window to predict the maneuver and spoof." "Flight time for these maneuvers was between 10-20 s." "For successful identification, UAV should complete the maneuver under 20 s and travel at-least 30 m from the original position"; "ArduCopter can provide accurate positioning with IMU measurements for 10 s"; a 20 m-wide "No-maneuver" zone is defined around the current trajectory; a straight deviation is rejected because "it is possible for an attacker to predict it". Abstract: "flight patterns that are less than 50 m long", recovery "within 10 seconds" on a Jetson Xavier. — [arXiv 2105.01860 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01860)
- Attacker model (§3.1): "The attacker can have omnidirectional or directional antennas. They can spoof any number of satellites. We do not restrict the position of the attacker. The attacker is aware of SemperFi and can craft spoofing signals accordingly. We assume that the attacker has not compromised the onboard sensors … The attacker is capable of executing the sophisticated seamless-takeover attack". — [arXiv 2105.01860 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01860)
- What the paper says about an attacker who knows/matches the manoeuvre (§6 Limitations): "For an attacker to stealthily circumvent SemperFi, the spoofing signals should reflect UAV's random maneuver to match the GPS derived motion with the IMU derived one. An attacker capable of generating spoofing signals in real-time based on the random maneuver will succeed, and the feasibility of executing such an attack remains to be studied. An attacker will have to predict the maneuver accurately to be able to evade peak identification. To do this, an attacker will have to track the drone in real-time with delays of only a few milliseconds … The refresh rate of typical GPS receivers provides the attacker about 200 ms to perform all these tasks." Also: the adopted detector "can reliably detect spoofing only if auxiliary peaks are visible." — [arXiv 2105.01860 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01860)
- The paper's own view of plain sensor-fusion detectors: "Prior works like [55] show the use of sensor fusion to detect spoofing attacks. However, these solutions may not provide reliable attack detection as an attacker can stealthly introduce spoofing signals." — [arXiv 2105.01860 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01860)
- GNSS-WASP on SemperFi and sensor fusion: "When a receiver receives signals from GNSS-WASP, the computed location is governed by the receiver's motion and there-by with the changing channel between the transmitter and the receiver. This ensures that the computed locations automatically align with the inertial measurements, thus defeating any security checks as long as the victims remain within the target area." and (Related Work) "Assuming that a receiver starts a trajectory from within the target area, its measured position would change consistently with its movements, and thus with other sensors like INS. Only the attack bias as discussed in Section 5 could eventually reveal the attack." — [GNSS-WASP PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity25/sec25cycle1-prepub-757-tibaldo.pdf)

### Inferences (short derivation — own work, consistent with the quoted procedure)
- Let the manoeuvre start at t_0 (after decoupling) and sample at t_k, k = 1..N. The IMU dead-reckoning track is p_IMU(t_k) = p̂(t_0) + ∫_{t_0}^{t_k} v(τ) dτ + e_IMU(t_k), where p̂(t_0) is the EKF state at decoupling and e_IMU is the drift (≤ 5 m budget for ≤ 20 s per the paper's calibration). The GPS track under attack is p_GPS(t_k) = x(t_k) + Δ(t_k) + n_k.
- API's statistic is built from "deviation samples", i.e. displacements relative to the manoeuvre start: δ_IMU(k) = p_IMU(t_k) − p_IMU(t_0), δ_GPS(k) = p_GPS(t_k) − p_GPS(t_0). Statistic: S = (1/N) Σ_k ‖δ_IMU(k) − δ_GPS(k)‖; alarm if S > 5 m.
- Constant offset, Δ(t) ≡ Δ: δ_GPS(k) = x(t_k) − x(t_0) + (n_k − n_0); Δ cancels identically, so S_attack = S_clean and the decision is unchanged — the manoeuvre's *shape* is preserved because a rigid translation commutes with differencing. (Even if API compared absolute tracks rather than displacements, p̂(t_0) was itself produced by the EKF while it still ingested spoofed GPS during the seamless takeover, so the EKF is already anchored at x(t_0)+Δ and the constant Δ again cancels. A constant Δ present before alignment is unobservable in either implementation.)
- Time-varying Δ(t): δ_GPS(k) acquires the extra term Δ(t_k) − Δ(t_0), so S_attack ≈ (1/N) Σ_k ‖Δ(t_k) − Δ(t_0)‖ (plus noise/drift). For a linear ramp Δ(t) = v·(t − t_0) over a manoeuvre of duration T_m, S ≈ v·T_m/2, giving a detectability floor v* ≈ 2·(5 m)/T_m ≈ 0.5 m/s for T_m = 20 s (1 m/s for 10 s) — in the same algebraic form as Park & Yoo's v* = 2γ/(1 − t_s/T). Ramps slower than ≈0.5 m/s during the manoeuvre, or any Δ(t) that is piecewise constant across each 10–20 s manoeuvre window, pass the test.
- Hence a coordinated attack that rigidly translates the whole swarm by a constant (or very slowly varying) Δ is in SemperFi's blind spot for two independent reasons: (a) the API statistic is translation-invariant by construction; (b) a distributed common-mode attack needs no real-time tracking of the manoeuvre (the condition the authors identify as the only escape), because the receiver's own motion through the spoofed field "automatically align[s] with the inertial measurements" (GNSS-WASP). A third practical reason: SemperFi's detector front-end only triggers the manoeuvre when auxiliary correlation peaks are visible; an attack that has completed takeover with the legitimate peak suppressed/merged (or that was present from cold start) never invokes API.
- Precisely what SemperFi *does* catch: the single-antenna, non-tracking spoofer whose counterfeit trajectory does not reproduce the manoeuvre (deviation ≈ full manoeuvre amplitude ≥ 30 m ≫ 5 m), and any attacker that must regenerate signals within ~200 ms.

### Gaps
- NDSS 2022 DOI 10.14722/ndss.2022.23184 and whether the NDSS version changed the 5 m threshold / manoeuvre timing: unverified (only the arXiv version was read).
- SemperFi does not state the formula of the deviation metric beyond the sentence quoted; the displacement form above is my reconstruction from "averaging the Euclidean distance between each deviation sample".

## KQ3. Other GNSS/INS consistency detectors (Tanil/Khanafseh/Pervan INS monitors, tightly-coupled innovation tests): what they detect and whether a constant offset applied before INS alignment is detectable

### Takeaway
The Illinois Tech family (Khanafseh et al. PLANS 2014; Tanil et al. TAES 2018; Kujur et al. NAVIGATION 2024) detects spoofing through Kalman-filter innovations of a tightly-coupled INS/GNSS filter; the innovation responds to *changes* in the GNSS measurement that the INS cannot explain (a step at onset, a ramp, or — for a tracking spoofer — the residual tracking noise). A position bias that is constant and already present when the filter is initialised/aligned is absorbed into the filter's position state and produces zero innovation (position is not observable from inertial data alone); the authors themselves frame the undetectable case as a spoofer with "unrealistic, near-perfect accuracy, and no delays", which is precisely what a distributed common-mode attack provides without tracking.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation: Çağatay Tanıl, Samer Khanafseh, Mathieu Joerger, Boris Pervan, "An INS Monitor to Detect GNSS Spoofers Capable of Tracking Vehicle Position," *IEEE Transactions on Aerospace and Electronic Systems*, vol. 54, no. 1, pp. 131–143, Feb. 2018, DOI 10.1109/TAES.2017.2739924 (article no. 8010424). Abstract (from the Arizona portal): the monitor "uses INS measurements to detect spoofing attacks on GNSS receivers" by monitoring "Kalman filter innovations in a tightly coupled INS/GNSS" system, tested "against worst-case attacks, including spoofers that can track the vehicle's position"; contributions include "an analytical expression for the worst-case sequence of spoofed signals"; application to a Boeing 747 on final approach; "The results show that GNSS spoofing is easily detected, with high integrity, unless the spoofer's position-tracking devices have unrealistic, near-perfect accuracy, and no delays." — [experts.arizona.edu](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/an-ins-monitor-to-detect-gnss-spoofers-capable-of-tracking-vehicl/); author PDF (link returned HTML during this research): [aoe.vt.edu](https://www.aoe.vt.edu/content/dam/aoe_vt_edu/people/faculty/joerger/publications/an_ins_monitor_to_detect_gnss_spoofers_capable_of_tracking_vehicle_position.pdf)

- Kujur et al. make the scope explicit: "To deliver a replica of the authentic signal to the target, the spoofer would need to know the position of the target's GNSS antenna … Consequently, the spoofer would need to track the target in real time. Any tracking errors would ultimately be embedded in the replica signal and appear to the target as additional 'noise' … As tracking errors are inevitable, detecting the presence of such unusual noise would expose active spoofing … we address this case specifically, as any additional offsets to the spoofing profile are irrelevant to the monitor." And a stated limitation of the CI monitor: "it does not offer a means for post-detection recovery because there is no fault-free source upon which to rely"; "In all prior performance evaluations, it was assumed that the monitor start time (conveniently) coincided with the spoofing onset time". — [Kujur 2024 PDF](https://navi.ion.org/content/navi/71/1/navi.629.full.pdf)
- The slow/constant case is recognised as the hard one across the tightly-coupled literature: a 2024 NWPU paper states tight coupling "is weak against slow-changing spoofing" and proposes measurement-averaged innovations — [pure.nwpu.edu.cn](https://pure.nwpu.edu.cn/en/publications/spoofing-detection-algorithm-for-tightly-coupled-insgnss-integrat) (full bibliographic data unverified); a BUAA paper ("Enhanced GNSS Spoofing Detector via Multiple-Epoch Inertial Navigation Sensor Prediction in a Tightly-Coupled System") reports that an innovation detector built on spoofed predictions "shows weak detection for slowly varying attacks" with long time-to-alarm — [research.buaa.edu.cn](https://research.buaa.edu.cn/en/publications/enhanced-gnss-spoofing-detector-via-multiple-epoch-inertial-navig/) (venue/year unverified); a *Sensors* 2022 (22(12):4503) paper describes a slowly varying spoofing method that pulls a loosely coupled GNSS/IMU solution while avoiding innovation-sequence monitoring — [mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/12/4503) (authors unverified); a *GPS Solutions* paper (DOI 10.1007/s10291-023-01594-3) proposes a weighted Kalman gain because the conventional EKF "does not account for spoofing's effect on innovation offsets" — [springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10291-023-01594-3) (title unverified).
- Park & Yoo's Appendix B (an onboard EKF-innovation monitor on a common-mode ramp) reports detection 20/20 at ramp rates 2–20 cm/s, but only 1/10 at 0.25 cm/s and 1/10 at 0.5 cm/s, 10/10 at 1.0 cm/s; they call it "a bare alarm: it recovers no true positions". — [arXiv 2608.06885](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06885v1)

### Inferences (short derivation)
- Linearised tightly/loosely coupled INS/GNSS error-state filter: δx = [δp, δv, δψ, b_a, b_g]. Inertial propagation gives δṗ = δv, δv̇ = −[f×]δψ + C b_a + …; the position error δp is a pure integrator with no feedback from the IMU, so a constant shift of p is in the unobservable subspace of the INS-only dynamics (it is observable *only* through the GNSS measurement z = Hp + …).
- Spoofed measurement z_k^s = z_k + H_k Δ_k (Kujur Eq. 5 with ν_k^t → Δ_k). Innovation: γ_k^s = γ_k + H_k (Δ_k − K-filtered memory of past Δ). Three regimes:
  1. **Δ constant and present at/before filter initialisation or alignment**: the filter's initial position estimate is p̂_0 = p_0 + Δ; all subsequent predictions carry Δ; γ_k^s = γ_k exactly → q_N unchanged → undetectable at any window length. (Equivalent statement: the filter is consistent with a world rigidly translated by Δ; nothing inertial distinguishes the two worlds.)
  2. **Step Δ applied after convergence**: γ_k jumps by H_kΔ at onset, then decays with the filter's time constant as the estimate re-converges; detectable iff ‖H_kΔ‖ exceeds the threshold in the transient window — this is the "jump" GNSS-WASP admits when a victim *enters* the spoofed area ("an isolated incident").
  3. **Ramp Δ_k = v·kΔt**: innovation carries a persistent bias ≈ H v (τ_f) where τ_f is the filter's effective lag; the CI monitor accumulates it; detectability improves with window length and IMU grade (Tanil's worst-case analysis), with a rate floor that can reach the cm/s level for good IMUs (Park & Yoo Appendix B: ~0.5–1 cm/s on their SITL setup).
- Therefore the INS family is *not* strictly a relative-geometry detector in space, but it is a **relative detector in time**: it checks consistency of increments (velocity/acceleration) and is blind to the time-constant component of Δ. A coordinated attack that imposes a constant common translation from cold start (or before the detector's start time, which Kujur et al. note was always assumed to coincide with onset) is in its null space; the Tanil monitor's escape condition ("near-perfect accuracy, no delays") is met by construction by a wide-area multi-transmitter spoofer, which needs no tracking at all.
- Velocity-domain cross-checks (GNSS Doppler vs INS-integrated velocity) are likewise invariant: d/dt of a constant Δ is zero. Only Δ̇ ≠ 0 is observable, and only above a rate floor set by IMU drift.

## KQ4. Detector classes that WOULD detect a rigid translation (absolute references) — to scope the thesis claim honestly

### Takeaway
A rigid common translation changes every receiver's *absolute* position by Δ; any check against an independent absolute reference whose signal path is outside the attacker's control (terrain/map/vision matching, cellular/Wi-Fi/Iridium positioning, known fixed reference receivers, trusted anchors) detects it with sensitivity set by that reference's accuracy — GNSS-WASP's authors concede "even an ideal attack impersonating the full constellation would be detected" that way. Signal-level monitors (C/N0, AoA, clock-drift, vestigial peaks) are orthogonal to geometry and may also fire, but they test the *signal*, not the translation itself, and a well-engineered multi-transmitter attack is designed to make them consistent.

### Inferences
- Honest scope statement for the thesis: the blindness result concerns the class D = f(relative quantities) — pairwise distances, multi-antenna baselines/attitude, inter-receiver clock-offset consistency, and GNSS/INS increment consistency (velocity/acceleration/manoeuvre shape). It does **not** extend to (i) absolute-position cross-checks against references with independent signal paths (cellular/Wi-Fi/Iridium/5G, terrain/map/vision, fixed surveyed receivers, trusted anchors, authenticated non-GNSS navigation), whose detection sensitivity is ‖Δ‖ versus the reference's accuracy (tens of metres for GSM/Wi-Fi, metres for RTK-anchored or vision over textured terrain); (ii) signal-level monitors, which may detect the *spoofing signal* (power, AoA, clock drift, auxiliary peaks) regardless of geometry — but these test the signal, not the translation, and a synchronized multi-transmitter attack with directional/altitude placement is explicitly designed to make them locally consistent (GNSS-WASP) at the price of a residual bias; (iii) the transient at attack onset or area entry (a step in Δ), which innovation-based filters can catch if Δ is not introduced before alignment or ramped below the rate floor; (iv) the approximation residual of a non-ideal wide-area spoof, detectable only with long-window statistics on a receiver array (GNSS-WASP §5).
- Anchor arithmetic: with ≥1 trusted absolute anchor the gauge is fixed and Δ is observable as the anchor residual; the minimum detectable ramp rate then scales as 2γ/(1 − t_s/T) with the anchor's own drift γ (Park & Yoo), which is the absolute-reference analogue of the 2·(5 m)/T_m floor derived for SemperFi above.

- IEEE DOI for Liu & Papadimitratos PLANS 2023, and bibliographic data for the terrain-matching patents and the Electronics 2025 vision paper, are unverified.
