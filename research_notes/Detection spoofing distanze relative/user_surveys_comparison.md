# Comparison of four user-supplied survey papers vs. the "multi-receiver relative-distance / relative-geometry" detector family

Scope note. This note analyses the four surveys the user supplied and asks, for each, whether the GNSS/GPS spoofing DETECTION techniques it covers coincide with, overlap with, or differ from the family of **relative-geometry consistency detectors** (pairwise distances between receivers in a known formation; multi-antenna baseline / double-difference carrier-phase checks; cooperative inter-platform ranging consistency; swarm-shape consistency; GNSS/INS consistency such as SemperFi or the Tanil/Pervan INS monitor). All statements about the papers are taken from the papers' own text (PDF text extracted with pdftotext; Psiaki-Humphreys Table 1 read from a rendered page image). Bibliographic data were cross-checked against Crossref.

## Key Question 1 — Paper 1 (= Paper 4): Altaweel, Mukkath, Kamel, IEEE Access 2023 (arnumber 10139317)

### Takeaway
This FANET-oriented systematic review (37 defence papers, 2017-2022) is the only one of the three surveys that actually contains members of the multi-receiver relative-geometry family (Hacohen 2020 inter-drone distance consistency; Liang 2022 and Han 2019 neighbour-position consistency; Kara 2021 leader-follower ranging), although it files them under "onboard devices", "signal processing" or "cryptographic" rather than under a geometry heading. It also explicitly describes the coordinated "spatial spoofing" attack on a swarm (Ceccato et al. 2020) and states, as an open research gap, that detectors relying on neighbours' shared positions fail when the neighbours are spoofed too — which is the rigid-translation blind spot in prose.

**(e) Coordinated / multi-transmitter / distributed spoofing and its detectability by consistency checks**
- Threat statement: "the spoofer has to counteract the swarm formation, i.e., the position of the FANET as a whole as well as the relative positions of UAVs within the swarm also has to be taken into account when spoofing attack is launched. A fake GPS signal relative to an individual drone will make obvious changes in the swarm formation and hence be detected easily." — Sec. II-G, p. 55241.
- The coordinated attack that defeats this: "This observation is considered by Ceccato et al. [56] in 2020 to design a spoofing tool against FANET to divert the routes of the entire swarm without disrupting the formation. This is called 'Spatial Spoofing', where FANETs move in the spoofed area in formation as if they are moving in their real target area. Multidimensional Linear Filters (e.g., Wiener Filter) are used for filtering the signals transmitted by the satellites in the target area and are further re-transmitted by multiple spoofing ground antennas. Ceccato et al. claimed that this releases the attacker from keeping track of each individual drone in the swarm." Citation: M. Ceccato, F. Formaggio, and S. Tomasin, "Spatial GNSS spoofing against drone swarms with multiple antennas and Wiener filter," *IEEE Trans. Signal Process.*, vol. 68, pp. 5782–5794, 2020. — p. 55247, ref. [56] p. 55278.
- Explicit statement of the blind spot of neighbour-consistency detectors: "any detection system that relies on the locational information shared by other drones will also get deceived by the wrong locational information provided by neighboring drones, which are also victims of the spoofing attack. For example, the solutions proposed in [71] and [87] leverage the GPS locational information shared by the neighboring drones. So, if these neighboring drones also fall in the spoofed area, the detection system fails ... The possibility that the entire swarm area to be spoofed and the minimum number of malicious nodes required for such spoofing also needs to be investigated. It is anticipated that defensive mechanisms might fail in such scenarios." — Sec. VI-A research gaps, p. 55257.
- Multi-attacker gap: "None of the papers covered in this study has investigated the number and type of tools that can be used to simultaneously launch the attack ... - Bada et al. [87] is the only detector the review labels as addressing "colluding" spoofing (multiple colluding spoofers/nodes), and it relies on received power and AoA witnesses, not geometry. — p. 55251.

### Inferences
- Hacohen et al. 2020 is a genuine member of the relative-distance family (measured inter-drone ranges vs. GNSS-derived ranges, Byzantine isolation) and is not in the user's reference list — it is the most important new candidate from these surveys.
- Liang 2022, Han 2019 and Kara 2021 are swarm/neighbour position-consistency checks: Han adds an independent relative bearing (RDF) and Kara an independent propagation-delay range, so they are relative-geometry detectors; Liang appears to compare only exchanged GPS fixes, which makes it the weakest member (no independent relative measurement).
- The review's own gap statement ("if these neighboring drones also fall in the spoofed area, the detection system fails") plus Ceccato's "spatial spoofing" together constitute, in this survey's vocabulary, exactly the rigid-translation null-space problem, although the review never formalises it.
- The review classifies by implementation basis, not by observable, so the relative-geometry family is scattered across three of its five categories; the user should not rely on its taxonomy headings to find the family.

### Gaps
- The review's Tables 11–14 (per-paper summary matrices) are rendered as images in the PDF and were not text-extractable; per-paper attacker-model and prerequisite fields were therefore taken from the body text only.
- The review does not cite Tippenhauer 2011, Jansen 2016, Swaszek 2013, Stenberg 2022, Heng 2015, Borio-Gioia 2016, Lee 2020, Psiaki multi-antenna, Montgomery 2009, Konovaltsev, SemperFi or Tanil/Pervan, so it provides no bridge to the classical multi-receiver literature.

## Key Question 2 — Paper 2: Psiaki & Humphreys, Proc. IEEE 2016

### Takeaway
A receiver-centric survey whose geometry-based class is **single-platform direction-of-arrival via multi-antenna or moving-antenna carrier phase (D10, D11)** plus IMU/clock drift monitoring (D4); it contains no multi-receiver or inter-platform distance-consistency detector. It is, however, the one survey that systematically rates multi-transmitter attacks (A9–A13), giving D10/D11 only "intermediate/case-dependent" effectiveness against them.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Bibliographic data (verified)**
- M. L. Psiaki and T. E. Humphreys, "GNSS Spoofing and Detection," *Proceedings of the IEEE*, vol. 104, no. 6, pp. 1258–1270, June 2016. DOI 10.1109/JPROC.2016.2526658. Invited paper; manuscript received 15 Oct. 2015, published 1 Apr. 2016. — PDF p. 1258; [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/JPROC.2016.2526658)

**(b) Taxonomy**

(B) Encryption-based — symmetric-key full-code encryption; dual-receiver cross-correlation of encrypted military code [10 Levin patent 2011; 11 O'Hanlon et al. 2013]; delayed symmetric-key SSSC [20 Scott 2003]; NMA [13 Kerns-Wesson-Humphreys 2014; 20]; SCER detection [21 Humphreys 2013]. (C) Drift monitoring — clock-offset drift vs. oscillator class; IMU / motion sensor / vehicle-constraint position-drift limits. (D) Signal-geometry-based — direction-of-arrival from beat carrier phase with three or more antennas [8 Konovaltsev et al. 2013], a single antenna with known motion [12 Psiaki-Powell-O'Hanlon 2013], two antennas [3 Psiaki et al. - Table 1 "Cost-Ranked Matrix" lists 13 detection techniques: D1 pseudorange-based RAIM; D2 observables and RPM; D3 correlation-function distortion monitoring; D4 drift monitoring (clock offset, IMU/position); D5 observables, RPM, distortion and drift monitoring; D6 NMA; D7 NMA and SCER detection; D8 delayed symmetric-key SSSC; D9 NMA, SCER detection, RPM and drift monitoring; D10 multiple RX antennas; D11 moving RX antenna; D12 dual-RX keyless correlation of unknown SSSC codes; D13 symmetric-key SSSC (e.g., P(Y)). **(c) Techniques in (or adjacent to) the RELATIVE-GEOMETRY family**
- D10 "Multiple RX antennas" / Sec. III-D: "A receiver can use interferometry to measure the direction-of-arrival vector R^i by using three or more antennas with different d offsets [8] ... If only two antennas are used ... at least one component will be estimable [3], [12]." Carrier-phase model (plain text): phi_i = rho_i0 + (R^i)^T d + c(delta_r − delta_i) + beta_i (eq. 4), where d is the antenna displacement from the nominal location. "A typical geometry-based spoofing detection system tests whether the received phi_i phases at the multiple antennas are more consistent with the diversity of R^i directions expected for authentic signals or with the uniformity of directions consistent with single-transmitter spoofing. The calculations for the nonspoofed hypothesis include an attitude determination algorithm [3], [8], [12]. One such system has demonstrated real-time spoofing detection aboard a cruising yacht [3]." Primary refs: [3] M. L. Psiaki et al., "GNSS lies, GNSS truth: Spoofing detection with two-antenna differential carrier phase," GPS World, vol. 25, no. 11, pp. 36–44, Nov. 2014; [8] A. Konovaltsev, M. Cuntz, C. Haettich, M. Meurer, "Autonomous spoofing detection and mitigation in a GNSS receiver with an adaptive antenna array," Proc. ION GNSS+, Nashville, 2013, pp. 2937–2948. — p. 1266, refs. p. 1269.
- D11 "Moving RX antenna": [12] M. L. Psiaki, S. P. Powell, B. W. O'Hanlon, "GNSS spoofing detection using high-frequency antenna motion and carrier-phase data," Proc. ION GNSS+, Nashville, 2013, pp. 2949–2991. — p. 1266, ref. p. 1269.
- IMU-aided carrier-phase geometry: "One such method uses an IMU on an aircraft [23]. In effect, motions induced by controls and winds produce a d(t) time history that can be sensed by the IMU but that is difficult for the spoofer to predict. If corresponding high-frequency variations consistent with each R^i direction are absent from each phi_i(t), then a spoofing attack is indicated." [23] C. Tanil, S. Khanafseh, B. Pervan, "GNSS spoofing attack detection using aircraft autopilot response to deceptive trajectory," Proc. ION GNSS+, Tampa, 2015, pp. 3345–3357. — p. 1266, ref. p. 1269.

- Dual-receiver correlation (D12/D13 family, [10], [11]): two receivers are involved ("done at a potential victim receiver and at another receiver that is known to be secure from spoofing"), but the consistency tested is the encrypted-code content, not geometry. — p. 1265.
- No technique in the paper compares positions or distances between separate receivers/platforms; the words "formation", "swarm", "pairwise distance" do not occur.

**(d) Techniques OUTSIDE the family**
- Observable-jump checks, RPM/AGC (D2), correlation-function distortion / SQM (D3), re-acquisition search for duplicate peaks, clock-drift monitoring (D4 clock part), NMA (D6), SCER detection (D7), SSSC (D8, D13), dual-receiver keyless correlation (D12), multi-feed single antenna [15] (DOA-like but single element). — pp. 1263–1266.

**(e) Coordinated / multi-transmitter / distributed spoofing**
- Attack description: "An advanced spoofer acting against a multiantenna victim receiver might use multiple independent spoofer transmission antennas and match each one to a corresponding receiver antenna. The relative geometry of each spoofer/victim antenna pair would need to be known. Also, the spoofer would need to be sufficiently close to the victim and have sufficiently narrow individual antenna gain patterns ... This type of spoofing would likely be practical only with a cooperative victim". "An expensive type of multiantenna spoofer might transmit only one spoofed signal from each antenna. Such a spoofer might deceive spoofing defenses that were based on signal direction of arrival. It might need to distribute its antennas about the victim ... The difficulty of mounting an attack from multiple directions would make this type of spoofer expensive and cumbersome." — Sec. II-D, p. 1262.
- "A spoofer that transmits from multiple directions is harder to detect using such methods. If its directional diversity does not equate to that of the true set of R^i vectors, then it may be detectable. The research group that produced [8] has developed a capability to detect such attacks." — Sec. III-D, p. 1266.
- Table 1 ratings (image): D10 "Multiple RX antennas" is rated high (check) against A1–A8 (all single-TX-antenna attacks) and intermediate/case-dependent (~) against A9 (RX/SP multi TX ants., no SCER), A10 (RX/SP multi TX, SCER), A11 (meaconing multi RX/multi TX), A12 (nulling multi TX, no SCER), A13 (nulling multi TX, SCER). D11 "Moving RX antenna" is high against A1–A7, low (X) against A8 (RX/SP sensing victim antenna motion) and intermediate against A9–A13. D4 drift monitoring is intermediate against every attack except A2 (open-loop simulator), where it is high. — Table 1, p. 1268.
- Section V: "a high-end spoofer might employ the receiver/spoofer architecture with nulling and SCER. It might use multiple transmission antennas carried on multiple platforms to create believable directions of arrival R^i. Receiver cross-correlation of military signals could defeat such a system ... Another effective defense would be NMA with SCER detection and clock drift monitoring. Most other defenses would fail against this attacker." — pp. 1268–1269.
- The paper also notes that spoofing a *cooperative* victim makes a "multi-transmission-antenna spoofer attack ... with a small array of antennas near the victim" easy, and recommends an opaque tamper-resistant radome "to conceal the antennas' relative geometry". — Sec. II-E, p. 1263.

**(f) Numbers**
- Carrier-phase precision and DOA accuracy: "A well-designed receiver typically can measure phi_i to an accuracy of about 1/40th of a cycle. This allows a receiver to measure R^i to an accuracy of about 3 degrees using only a short baseline of d = 0.1 m." — p. 1266.
- RPM: "a sudden power jump could indicate an attack, especially if the increase were more than 1 or 2 dB." — p. 1264.
- No Pd/Pfa values are given for D10/D11; Table 1 uses only the qualitative scale high / intermediate / low. — p. 1268.

### Inferences
- The "Psiaki multi-antenna" reference in the user's list corresponds here to refs. [3] (two-antenna differential carrier phase, GPS World 2014) and [12] (antenna motion, ION GNSS+ 2013); Konovaltsev/DLR corresponds to ref. [8]; Tanil/Pervan to ref. [23]. All three are in the user's list, so this survey adds no new relative-geometry references, but it supplies the canonical formulation (eq. 4) and the 1/40-cycle / 3-degree / 0.1 m numbers.
- D10/D11 test the diversity of satellite arrival directions on a single platform. A single-transmitter coordinated attack that rigidly translates a whole formation is still caught by D10, because all spoofed signals share one DOA; the only attacks against which D10 degrades (A9–A13) are per-antenna multi-transmitter attacks, which the authors describe as expensive and practical mainly with a cooperative victim. The rigid-translation null-space argument therefore does not apply to D10/D11; what applies is a different, geometric-diversity argument (the spoofer must reproduce N independent arrival directions).
- D4 (IMU drift monitoring) is a per-platform absolute-dynamics check; its weakness against a slow translation is a threshold/rate argument ("patient spoofer"), not the relative-geometry null space.

## Key Question 4 — Synthesis: are the detection techniques in these papers the same as the relative-distance family?

### Takeaway
Mostly no. Across the three distinct surveys (paper 4 duplicates paper 1), the overwhelming majority of detectors are signal-level (power, SQM, correlation peaks), cryptographic, ML/DL on receiver features, or per-platform absolute cross-checks (INS, vision, WiFi, radar, cellular, ADS-B MLAT). Only the FANET review (Altaweel et al. 2023) contains true multi-receiver relative-geometry detectors — Hacohen 2020 (inter-drone distances), Han 2019 (RDF bearings vs. shared positions), Kara 2021 (leader-follower ranging), Liang 2022 (shared-position consistency) — and only Psiaki-Humphreys 2016 covers the multi-antenna carrier-phase/DOA branch (D10/D11, refs. [3], [8], [12], [23]). None of the surveys cites Tippenhauer 2011, Jansen 2016, Swaszek 2013, Stenberg 2022, Heng 2015, Borio-Gioia 2016, Lee 2020, Montgomery 2009 or SemperFi; Jansen 2018 (Crowd-GPS-Sec) appears in Altaweel et al. and, as a baseline figure only, in Sebastian & Manjith.
