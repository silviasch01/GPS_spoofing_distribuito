# Position-domain GNSS spoofing detection with multiple receivers in a known formation (2011–2026)

Scope: detectors that compare the *reported positions / pairwise distances* of several civilian GNSS receivers (static arrays, vehicles, UAV swarms) against their known geometry. Measurement-domain multi-receiver methods (double differences of pseudorange / carrier phase) are included only where the assignment named them (Stenberg et al.) or where they are needed to frame the attacker model.

Notation convention used throughout: P_i (or z_i) = reported position of receiver i; d_ij = ||P_i − P_j|| = distance between reported positions; D_ij = known physical distance; λ, T = decision threshold; H0 = authentic, H1 = spoofed (as used by each paper — note Jansen et al. define H0/H1 this way but Stenberg et al. invert the roles).

Bibliographic fields were cross-checked on at least two sources (paper PDF + Crossref/ION/URI record) unless marked "unverified".

## KQ1 — Tippenhauer, Pöpper, Rasmussen, Čapkun, "On the Requirements for Successful GPS Spoofing Attacks" (ACM CCS 2011)

### Takeaway
The paper formalises the "GPS Group Spoofing Problem" and shows that a single-antenna attacker can only move *all* receivers to one and the same location (Result 1), so two receivers that compare their reported positions already defeat it; a multi-antenna attacker can preserve a formation, but for n ≥ 5 victims the admissible antenna positions collapse to a single point per impersonated satellite (the affine image T^{-1} L^A_i of the claimed satellite position), which the authors turn into a countermeasure based on checking that the reported positions preserve the known formation. The test is purely *relative* (formation preservation); the authors themselves prove (Result 7) that a formation-preserving attack — a rigid translation+rotation of all receivers — always has at least one solution.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation (verified via Crossref and the PDF front page): Nils Ole Tippenhauer, Christina Pöpper, Kasper B. Rasmussen, Srdjan Čapkun, "On the requirements for successful GPS spoofing attacks," *Proceedings of the 18th ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security (CCS '11)*, Chicago, IL, Oct. 17–21, 2011, pp. 75–86, DOI 10.1145/2046707.2046719. Open-access author PDF: https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/2046707.2046719); [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Attacker model (Sect. 3.3): "The attacker controls a set of wireless transmitters that he can move and position independently", located at P^A_i ∈ R^3 (one transmission unit per impersonated satellite S_i), with chosen time shift δ^A_i; attacker is assumed to know the victims' physical locations; all attacker signals are received by all victims (Fig. 3a); the model is explicitly "very strong … worst-case". — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Core equations (plain text): expected pseudorange of victim j from attacker antenna i: `R^A_ij = |P_j − P^A_i| + δ^A_i · c` (Eq. 6); victim solves `|L'_j − L^A_i| = R'_ij − Δ'_j` (Eq. 7); success requires `|P_j − P^A_i| + Δ^A_i = |L'_j − L^A_i| + Δ'_j` (Eq. 8); pseudorange differences `b_ijk = R^A_ij − R^A_ik = |P_j − P^A_i| − |P_k − P^A_i|` (Eq. 11). — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Result 1 (single antenna): "One or more receivers V_j ∈ V can be spoofed to any one location L' using a single attacker antenna. Spoofing multiple receivers to the same location L' will generally lead to different time offsets δ'_j at each victim." Reason: all victims obtain the same TDoAs between satellite signals. — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Result 3 (necessary condition): "∀V_j, V_k, ∀s_i, b'_ijk ≤ |P_j − P_k|", i.e. `|P_j − P_k| ≥ |L'_j − L^A_i| − |L'_k − L^A_i| + Δ'_j − Δ'_k` (Eq. 13). — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Results 2, 4, 5, 6 (multi-antenna attacker, formation preserved): with 2 victims the admissible P^A_i lie on (one sheet of) a hyperboloid; 3 victims → intersection curve of two hyperboloids; 4 victims → at most 2 points; ≥ 5 victims → at most one point (intersection of n−1 hyperboloids b'_i12,…,b'_i1n; "(n−1) linear equations and one quadratic equation is overdetermined"). Table 2 summarises: spoofing to one location is unrestricted (P^A_i ∈ R^3) for any n; for preserved formation civilian GPS gives "set of hyperboloids / set of intersections / set of 2 points / set of points", military GPS gives "one hyperboloid / intersection / 2 points / 1 point". — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Result 7 (existence of a formation-preserving attack): "When spoofing a group of GPS receivers V_1,…,V_n such that the formation (i.e., the mutual distances and relative time offsets) is preserved, there is always at least one solution to the decisional group GPS spoofing problem." Proof sketch: if the formation is preserved there is a bijective affine (translation+rotation) matrix T with T·L_j = L'_j; then P^A_i = T^{-1}·L^A_i is a valid antenna placement because the transformation preserves Euclidean distances. "As a consequence of Results 6 and 7, spoofing five or more receivers while retaining their formation has exactly one solution, an affine transformation of the claimed satellite position L^A_i." — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Countermeasure (Sect. 6): receivers "deployed in a static, known formation, e.g., … fixed on the deck of a cargo ship"; "If the GPS receivers can exchange their individual GPS locations, they can check if their calculated locations preserve their physical formation (within certain error bounds). In the case that the calculated GPS locations do not match the known formation, an attack must be suspected." No explicit statistic, threshold or error model is given ("within certain error bounds"). — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Single vs multi-antenna claim: "Even if only two GPS receivers are used, this countermeasure can detect any attacker that is only using a single antenna … both GPS receivers would report the same location (with small time offsets)." "A strong attacker using multiple antennas could attempt to send signals such that the mutual distances between multiple receivers are preserved. Nevertheless, each additional receiver of the victim makes these spoofing attacks exceedingly more difficult … From Results 6 and 7 we know that there exists only one location per satellite where the attacker can place his antenna; this location is the rotated and translated satellite position." Fig. 10 caption: "For an attacker with a single antenna, the two-receiver countermeasure is enough. If the attacker uses multiple antennas, four (or more) receivers severely restrict the attacker's antenna placements." — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- On "n receivers need n antennas": the paper's model has one attacker antenna *per impersonated satellite* (P^A_i indexed by satellite i), not per victim; it only says that attacking each victim with a dedicated, shielded/directional antenna (Fig. 3b) would make "the number of required attacker antennas … linear in the number of victims" and is dismissed as impractical when receivers are close. — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Practical lock-takeover bounds (Sect. 5, Table 3): constant time offset must be < 75 ns (≈ 22.5 m) and relative offset between antennas < 80 ns, "if an attacker has multiple antennas, he must precisely know the distance from each antenna to the [victim]". Becomes "impossible if the victim can hide the exact positioning of at least one GPS receiver from the attacker (e.g., by keeping it mobile on the vehicle)". — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Assumptions: 3-D analysis in the group-spoofing theory; countermeasure assumed static formation; mobile settings "with varying formations (e.g., mobile formation of cars, robots)" need secure ranging to establish the formation — left as future work; no noise model, no Pd/Pfa, no minimum spacing given (only "if the distances between the receivers are small" the per-victim antenna option is impractical). — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)
- Relative vs absolute: the countermeasure checks only whether "calculated locations preserve their physical formation"; nothing absolute is used. — [PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6489/gps.pdf)

### Inferences
- Result 7 is precisely the statement that a rigid motion of the whole formation lies in the null space of the formation-preservation test: for *any* n the attacker has ≥ 1 solution, and for n ≥ 5 exactly one antenna position per satellite. The paper's security argument is therefore about *attack difficulty* (constrained antenna placement, ns-level timing), not about *undetectability* of a formation-preserving attack by the relative test.
- The oft-quoted "n receivers need n antennas" is not literally in the paper; what it says is (i) one antenna per spoofed satellite (≥ 4) for the coordinated attack, with placement uniquely determined for n ≥ 5, or (ii) one shielded antenna per victim for the trivial per-victim attack.

### Gaps
- The paper gives no quantitative detector, threshold, or Pd/Pfa; those appear only in follow-up work (Swaszek et al., Jansen et al.).

## KQ2 — Jansen, Tippenhauer, Pöpper, "Multi-Receiver GPS Spoofing Detection: Error Models and Realization" (ACSAC 2016)

### Takeaway
Defines the test `f({d_ij}_{i<j}) ≷ λ` on the set of pairwise distances between reported positions (spoofing declared when f < λ), models d_ij as Rician under H0 and Rayleigh under H1 with a spatially correlated 2-D Gaussian position error (`σ_d = sqrt(2)·σ·sqrt(1−ρ)`), measures σ ≈ 0.5–3 m, ρ ≈ 0.4–0.6 (authentic) vs σ ≈ 0.2–1 m, ρ ≈ 0.5–0.9 (spoofed), and concludes that four receivers on a 5 m square (r ≈ 3.5 m) suffice instead of 10–50 m; the "normalized majority" variant of f is best. The test is purely relative; the paper acknowledges the multi-antenna attacker but relies on Tippenhauer et al. for the claim that m ≥ 4 receivers make it infeasible.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation (verified: Crossref + PDF front page): Kai Jansen, Nils Ole Tippenhauer, Christina Pöpper, "Multi-Receiver GPS Spoofing Detection: Error Models and Realization," *Proceedings of the 32nd Annual Conference on Computer Security Applications (ACSAC '16)*, Los Angeles, CA, Dec. 5–9, 2016, ACM, pp. 237–250, DOI 10.1145/2991079.2991092, ISBN 978-1-4503-4771-6. The DOI 10.1145/2991079.2991092 is confirmed (Crossref returns title "Multi-receiver GPS spoofing detection", pages 237-250, authors Jansen/Tippenhauer/Pöpper, issued 2016-12-05). Open-access PDF: https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf (the older link www.tippenhauer.de/papers/jansen2016multi.pdf currently returns a TLS/503 error). — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/2991079.2991092); [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf); [CISPA record](https://cispa.de/en/research/publications/67452-multi-receiver-gps-spoofing-detection-error-models-and-realization)
- Hypotheses and generic test (Sect. 3.1): "H0: No spoofing occurred. H1: Spoofing is performed." Test (1): `f(P_1,…,P_m) ≷ λ` (H0 above, H1 below) where "f() is a function on the distances. Each position P_i consists of a latitude and a longitude component. The position also contains altitude information, which is neglected here due to the low precision of GPS altitude … we assume that for our countermeasure all receivers are placed at approximately the same height." — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Relative-only statement and Eq. (2): "Since we only consider the relative distances between receivers, we can detail (1) to directly take the set of distances d_{i,j} as input: `f(d_{i,j}) := f({d_{i,j}}_{1≤i<j≤m}) ≷ λ` (2). If the result of function f … falls below the threshold λ, the test indicates a spoofing attack (H1). However, if the result is above the threshold λ, the test decides for no spoofing (H0). Notably, since the absolute positions contained in (1) are not crucial for our spoofing detection, there is no information loss from (1) to (2)." Also: "In contrast to the absolute positions in [27] [= Swaszek & Hartnett ITM 2014], our detection is based on relative distances between all pairs of receivers." — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Probabilities as printed: `p_d = Pr{f(d_ij) < λ | H1}`, `p_fa = Pr{f(d_ij) < λ | H0}` (1 ≤ i < j ≤ m). — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Formation (Sect. 3.2): m receivers equidistant on a circle of radius r; neighbour distance `d = 2r·sin(2π/(2m))`; m = 2 → d = 2r; m = 4 → square; "for m = 4 we already have six (partially dependent) distances"; m = 4 chosen as "the least amount of receivers required while protecting against the multiple-antenna attacker [29]". — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Error model (Sect. 3.4): position errors Gaussian in lat/lon with equal σ; 1-D illustration `d(P_i + n_i, P_j + n_j) = d(P_i, P_j) + n_i − n_j`; `σ_d = sqrt(σ_i^2 + σ_j^2 − 2 ρ_ij σ_i σ_j) = (σ_i = σ_j) sqrt(2)·σ·sqrt(1 − ρ_ij)`, with Pearson `ρ_{X,Y} = cov(X,Y)/(σ_X σ_Y)` (Eq. 3). Distance between a 2-D Gaussian point and a fixed point is Rician: PDF `f(x) = (x/σ^2)·exp(−(x^2+s^2)/(2σ^2))·I_0(x s/σ^2)`, x > 0 (Eq. 4); CDF `F(x) = 1 − Q_1(s/σ, x/σ)` (Eq. 5, Marcum Q); replacing the fixed point by a second correlated Gaussian point "maintains its Rician property" (s, σ then "very close to the actual scales"). Under spoofing both centres coincide (s = 0) → Rayleigh: `f(x) = (x/σ^2)·exp(−x^2/(2σ^2))` (Eq. 6), `F(x) = 1 − exp(−x^2/(2σ^2))` (Eq. 7). Distances are "projected on a two-dimensional plane neglecting the curvature of the earth for small distances". — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Threshold rules derived from the models: authentic case `Pr{d ≤ d99} = 1 − Q_1(s/σ, d99/σ)` with d99 = distance exceeded 99 % of the time; spoofed case `Pr{d > d99} = exp(−d99^2/(2σ^2))`. Simulation threshold λ is chosen at the EER, `1 − p_d = p_fa` (Eq. 8), or by fixing p_d = 0.99 and reading off p_fa; the prototype threshold "is an estimation that optimizes both the detection and the false alarm probability". — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Measured authentic parameters (Table 3; four Arduino/GPS-shield receivers, ≈ 2.5 h, clear sky): per pair (s, σ, d99, ρ_LAT, ρ_LON): R1-R2 (8.129, 0.681, 6.576, 0.045, 0.399); R1-R3 (13.319, 0.809, 11.464, 0.490, 0.779); R1-R4 (10.802, 0.779, 9.020, 0.509, 0.472); R2-R3 (7.045, 0.796, 5.244, 0.721, 0.654); R2-R4 (11.337, 1.132, 8.768, 0.511, 0.462); R3-R4 (9.760, 1.495, 6.423, 0.351, 0.719). Mean distance error μ 0.79 m (R4) to 1.61 m (R3). Conclusion: "σ ≈ 0.5,…,3 … ρ ≈ 0.4,…,0.6 for long-term measurements". Appendix B: σ ≈ 0.746 to 3.063 (near a metallic wall); ρ down to 0.265 (wall reflections) and up to 0.820. — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Measured spoofed parameters (Table 4; LabSat 3 simulator, indoor, single antenna): (σ, d99, ρ_LAT, ρ_LON): R1-R2 (0.126, 0.381, 0.970, 0.932); R1-R3 (0.137, 0.416, 0.975, 0.916); R1-R4 (0.216, 0.655, 0.870, 0.898); R2-R3 (0.092, 0.279, 0.986, 0.969); R2-R4 (0.160, 0.487, 0.932, 0.964); R3-R4 (0.181, 0.550, 0.927, 0.959). μ ≈ 0.47–0.57 m, σ ≈ 0.21–0.29 m per receiver; "σ ≈ 0.2,…,1 … ρ ≈ 0.5,…,0.9"; Appendix B worst cases σ ≈ 0.882, ρ from 0.981 down to 0.463. — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Simulation cases (Table 5: σ_legit, ρ_legit, σ_spoof, ρ_spoof): Case 1 (4, 0.5, 2, 0.5); Case 2 (2, 0.5, 1, 0.5); Case 3 (1, 0.5, 1, 0.5); Case 4 (1, 0.5, 1, 0.7); Case 5 (1, 0.5, 0.5, 0.9); m = 4, r swept 0–15 m in 0.01 m steps, 10^7 samples per point. EER results (Fig. 9): radius ≈ 11 m for EER = 10^-6 in Case 1; ≈ 6 m (Case 2), ≈ 4 m (Case 3), ≈ 3.5 m (Case 4), ≈ 2 m (Case 5). — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Comparison with Swaszek's σ = 4 m (Fig. 10): with p_d fixed at 0.99 and σ = 4, ρ = 0.5 for both hypotheses, p_fa = 10^-5 needs r ≈ 12.31 m; with σ_legit = 4, σ_spoof = 1, ρ_legit = 0.5, ρ_spoof = 0.9 the same p_fa needs r ≈ 3.63 m, i.e. a square of edge ≈ 5.13 m (≈ 26.32 m² vs 200 m² for a 14 m square in [27]). — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Appendix A, variants of f (m = 4, six distances): "minimal and the maximal functions only consider the minimal, respectively the maximal, measured distance from the set of all distances. The majority approach … decides for spoofing when the majority of distances, i.e., four out of six, fall below the decision threshold. The normalized approach makes some distances more significant than others, e.g., the diagonal in a square is sqrt(2) times longer than the edges and then performs a majority voting." Table 6 (relative EER averaged over radii, Case 3; lower is better): Minimal ≥ 7 / ≥ 9 / ≥ 10; Maximal 1.0000 / 1.2049 / 1.2344; Majority 0.8457 / 1.0000 / 1.0224; Normalized 0.8320 / 0.9820 / 1.0000. "the normalized approach performs approx. 2 % better than the (non-normalized) majority voting and approx. 17 % better than the maximal function"; "the (normalized) majority voting approach is the optimal choice for all considered error models"; the minimal function "offers the worst performance". — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Prototype (Sect. 7): m = 4 receivers on a square of edge d = 5.00 m (r ≈ 3.54 m), two receivers near a metal wall; ≈ 3 h, > 80,000 fixes; normalized majority f; "no false alarms … detected the spoofing attack reliably"; statistic is "constantly above the threshold" authentic and "always below" under spoofing (Fig. 12). — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Attacker model (Sect. 2.3): single-antenna attacker (all signals from one source → identical position at every victim) vs multi-antenna attacker ("can send potentially different signals from various locations"); "all receivers obtain signals from the same sources, i.e., receivers are not shielded"; lock take-over out of scope; attacker is granted the ability to defeat RAIM, power and AoA checks. — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Multi-antenna / formation-preserving attacker (Sect. 8): "The multi-antenna attacker utilizes (at least) four antennas each sending out a different satellite signal … if we position the antennas such that the ToA at an adjacent receiver can be correctly resolved to a position that is a configurable distance apart, we can realize a distance-preserving multi-antenna attack." Their own 4×USRP N210 + gps-sdr-sim attempt spoofed one receiver but failed on two because of ToA re-arrangement and power imbalance (antenna distances to receiver 2 from 0.5 m to 7 m → "very unstable lock"); they note power differences shrink for a 100 m-distant attacker. Conclusion: "For settings with m ≥ 4 receivers, a multi-antenna attack … cannot preserve the relative distances of all receivers [29]. As a result, our proposed multi-receiver countermeasure with four receivers is expected to be resilient against multi-antenna attacks by design." — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Related prior quantitative claims reported by Jansen et al.: Heng et al. (PECI 2014, ref [5]) "derived performance values for mutual distances of 20 m achieving a false rejection rate of less than 0.1 and a false detection rate of 0.01 (location noise σ = 5 m)"; Swaszek & Hartnett [27]: "a square setup with 14 m edge distance would achieve a false acceptance rate of ≈ 10^-5 and a detection rate of ≈ 0.99 (location noise σ = 4 m)". — [PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)

### Inferences
- The test depends on the reported positions only through {d_ij}; any map P_i ↦ R·P_i + t with R a rotation and t a translation leaves f unchanged, so a formation-preserving (rigid) attack is in its null space. The paper never claims otherwise; its resilience claim to multi-antenna attackers is inherited from Tippenhauer et al.'s *feasibility* argument (and from the ns-level synchronisation and power constraints shown in Sect. 8), not from the statistic itself.
- Note the inversion of the usual convention: Jansen et al. put the attack in H1 and declare H1 when f < λ (distances shrink toward 0 under a single-antenna attack), so "p_fa" is the probability that authentic distances fall below λ.

### Gaps
- The paper reports EER/p_fa curves as figures; the exact thresholds λ used in the prototype are not tabulated. No formal treatment of time-series (sliding-window) decisions, only mentioned as possible.

## KQ3 — Jansen, Schäfer, Moser, Lenders, Pöpper, Schmitt, "Crowd-GPS-Sec" (IEEE S&P 2018)

### Takeaway
Crowd-GPS-Sec is *not* a known-formation detector: it uses crowdsourced ground sensors (OpenSky) to (Test 1) cross-check each aircraft's ADS-B/Flarm-reported GPS position against an independent TDoA-multilateration position (an *absolute* reference) and (Test 2) flag two aircraft whose reported positions are closer than the separation minima (`dist(â_i, â_j) > T2?`), a *relative* test that exploits the single-antenna collapse to one location; detection rate ≈ 75 % (spoofer directly beneath an aircraft above 11 km), < 2 s delay, spoofer localisation ≈ 150 m after 15 min.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation (verified via Crossref and the RPTU library record): Kai Jansen, Matthias Schäfer, Daniel Moser, Vincent Lenders, Christina Pöpper, Jens B. Schmitt, "Crowd-GPS-Sec: Leveraging Crowdsourcing to Detect and Localize GPS Spoofing Attacks," *2018 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (SP)*, San Francisco, CA, May 2018, pp. 1018–1031, DOI 10.1109/SP.2018.00012, ISBN 978-1-5386-4353-2 / 978-1-5386-4354-9 (electronic), ISSN 2375-1207. Bronze open access PDF: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/SP.2018.00012); [RPTU record](https://biblio.ub.rptu.de/frontdoor/index/index/year/2024/docId/12133); [OpenAlex OA link](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/SP.2018.00012)
- Independent localisation (Sect. III-A): sensors at known positions, GPS-time-synchronised; `dist(s_i, A) − dist(s_j, A) = Δt_{i,j} · c` (Eq. 3), "By construction of at least four relations of this type, we perform multilateration". — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)
- Time alignment: transmission time `t_TX = t_s − dist(s,a)/c` (Eq. 4) and linear interpolation of reported positions to a common reference time t_REF. — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)
- Test 1 (cross-check with MLAT): `dist(a_i, â_i) <? T1` (Eq. 5), a_i = MLAT position, â_i = reported GPS position, "T1 denotes a predefined threshold which tolerates measurement errors … Smaller T1 lead to higher false positive rates, while larger T1 create more room for undetected manipulations." Complexity O(n). — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)
- Test 2 (multiple aircraft comparison): "When multiple aircraft receive the signals from the same spoofer device, they will appear at the same location [7] since the time differences between individual satellites are emulated on the radio of the spoofer prior transmission. Due to mandatory separation minima … `dist(â_i, â_j) = d_{i,j} >? T2` (Eq. 6) … T2 is a threshold tolerating the GPS positioning noise … a threshold as small as a few hundreds of meters is appropriate." Complexity O(n²), reducible via k-d/cover trees. — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)
- Threshold setting: empirical / operational (MLAT accuracy for T1; separation minima + ADS-B accuracy for T2); no closed-form distribution. — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)
- Attacker model for the evaluation: omnidirectional single antenna "positioned right underneath the target using exactly the required transmission power"; spherical attack range. — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)
- Performance: Central-Europe OpenSky data, 1 h, 141,693 positions of 142 aircraft; "if the spoofer's target is at an altitude above 11 km and the spoofer is directly underneath the target, the detection rate is about 75 % using both tests. If the spoofer uses higher transmission powers or if it is not directly underneath the target, the detection rate increases quickly towards 100 %"; Test 2 delay ≈ 0.5 s (ADS-B broadcast interval 0.4–0.6 s), Test 1 slower (MLAT latency); abstract: detection "in less than two seconds", spoofer localised "up to an accuracy of 150 meters after 15 minutes of monitoring time". — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)
- Multi-transmitter attacker: not analysed; the evaluation assumes one omnidirectional spoofer per target. — [PDF](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/8418581/8418583/08418651.pdf)

### Inferences
- Test 2 is a pairwise-distance test with the same null space as Jansen 2016 (invariant to a common rigid shift of all reported positions), but Test 1 is anchored to an absolute, GNSS-independent reference (TDoA MLAT), which is exactly what the thesis argument says is needed to observe a rigid translation.

### Gaps

## KQ4 — Swaszek, Hartnett and co-authors (ION ITM 2013, ION GNSS+ 2013, ION ITM 2014, ION GNSS+ 2017)

### Takeaway

## KQ5 — Stenberg, Axell, Rantakokko, Hendeby, "Results on GNSS Spoofing Mitigation Using Multiple Receivers" (NAVIGATION 69(1), 2022)

### Takeaway
A measurement-domain (not position-domain) multi-receiver method: GLRT on double differences of pseudorange (chi-square with r = 2 d.o.f., or F-distribution when variance is unknown) or carrier phase (r = 1), under a single-transmitter attacker; the ≈ 5 m minimum spacing is empirical (sporadic wrong authentications at 5 m with 10 s windows; good from 10 m; 6/7 pseudoranges authenticated within 30 s at 20 m). The test uses only inter-receiver differences (relative), but authenticated measurements are then fed to an ordinary absolute PVT.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation: Niklas Stenberg, Erik Axell, Jouni Rantakokko, Gustaf Hendeby, "Results on GNSS Spoofing Mitigation Using Multiple Receivers," *NAVIGATION: Journal of the Institute of Navigation*, vol. 69, no. 1, article navi.510, March 2022, DOI 10.33012/navi.510 (open access; HTML and PDF at navi.ion.org). — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/b3d9497a516342ecaadef30c0e2407fb)
- Predecessors: Stenberg (2019), "Spoofing mitigation using multiple GNSS-receivers," M.Sc. thesis, Linköping University; Stenberg, Axell, Rantakokko, Hendeby (2020), "GNSS spoofing mitigation using multiple receivers," *2020 IEEE/ION PLANS*, Portland, OR, DOI 10.1109/PLANS46316.2020.9109958. — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510)
- Hypotheses (note the inversion relative to Jansen): H0 = all measurements in the double difference come from the spoofer; H1 = at least one is authentic. Pseudorange DD under H0 modelled as a first-order polynomial in time plus white Gaussian noise; carrier-phase DD under H0 as a constant offset plus noise (constant ambiguities). Stacked linear batch model, pre-whitened, GLRT. Known variance → threshold from a chi-square distribution with r = 2 (pseudorange) or r = 1 (carrier phase) d.o.f. (Detectors 1a/2a); unknown variance → F distribution with r and N−p d.o.f. (Detectors 1b/2b; 1b used for the main pseudorange results). — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510)
- Mitigation logic: test every DD for all satellite pairs, count flags per measurement, remove measurements flagged ≥ K−1 times with K = 4; "The K = 4 rule relies on the single-transmitter assumption: flagged signals must share a source, and at least four spoofed signals are needed for a PVT fix." — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510)
- Why ≈ 5 m: "The paper does not derive the 5 m figure. It is an empirical result from simulations. The pseudorange method 'performed well for receiver distances larger than or equal to 5 meters.' At 5 m with a 10 s window, there were sporadic erroneous authentications. Carrier phase can work at shorter spacing but needs better time synchronization … decimeter-level spacing could be theoretically possible, since the L1 C/A wavelength is about 2 dm." Abstract: "mitigation using pseudoranges is possible in these tests when the receivers are separated by five meters or more. At 20 meters, the pseudorange algorithm correctly authenticates six out of seven pseudoranges within 30 seconds". — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510)
- Other reported performance: Spirent GSS9000, 7 authentic + 7 spoofed signals; most signals authenticated within 30 s for spacing ≥ 10 m; zero incorrect authentications except the 5 m case; spoofing power −3/+3/+10/+20 dB → Detector 1b ≈ 7 correct authentications on average; carrier-phase theoretical thresholds "did not perform well", so thresholds set empirically at 1 % false alarm; live-sky meaconing (Vidsel 2019), pairs ≈ 50 m and ≈ 15 m, 15 s windows, mitigated solutions from ≈ 09:27:00 at J/N 2.7 dB / 0.8 dB; detection-only mode: the ≈ 15 m pair flagged all 8 common satellites over 27 min with 30 s windows. — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510)
- Assumptions: single spoofer transmit antenna (meaconing or self-consistent spoofer, fixed delay); R ≥ 2 receivers each tracking both authentic and spoofed signals for the same satellites; time synchronisation ≈ 1 ms (pseudorange) / < 1 µs (carrier phase); no multipath; stationary receivers; mobile use "harder and only partly applicable". Multiple spoofer transmitters: not analysed; extension "straightforward" but costlier. — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510)
- Relative vs absolute: "Detection uses only inter-receiver double differences … the paper does not require a known baseline for the test. Absolute position comes from a separate PVT computation (RTKLIB) on the authenticated measurements." — [navi.ion.org](https://navi.ion.org/content/69/1/navi.510)

### Inferences
- Because the detector keys on the *temporal behaviour* of DDs under a single transmitter (all spoofed DDs share one geometry), a coordinated multi-transmitter attacker that reproduces satellite-like DD geometry at both receivers would fall outside the assumed model; the paper does not claim robustness against it.

### Gaps
- Equation rendering was stripped from the fetched HTML; the exact matrix form of the GLRT statistic should be copied from the PDF (navi.510.full-text.pdf) if needed verbatim.

## KQ6 — Other position-domain multi-receiver / relative-geometry works (2011–2026)

### Takeaway
At least six further works fit the family: Heng et al. (PECI 2014, power-grid timing), Kalantari & Larsson (EURASIP JASP 2020, GLRT for a rigid body with unknown pose and *multiple* spoofers), Mykytyn et al. (MECO 2023, UAV swarm |d_GPS − d_UWB| > threshold), Michieletto et al. (IEEE T-ASE 2023, UAV formation with RSS ranging), Bi et al. (arXiv 2023 / IEEE TIFS, SDP localisation-feasibility test), and two attack papers that directly address formation-preserving spoofing — GNSS-WASP (USENIX Security 2025) and Park & Yoo's RigidShift (arXiv 2608.06885, 2026) — the latter stating explicitly that any detector that is a function only of relative quantities is invariant under a common translation.

### Cited Findings
- Heng, Makela, Domínguez-García, Bobba, Sanders, Gao, "Reliable GPS-Based Timing for Power Systems: A Multi-Layered Multi-Receiver Architecture," *Power and Energy Conference at Illinois (PECI '14)*, IEEE, 2014 (pages/DOI unverified). As summarised by Jansen et al.: position-comparison layer, "mutual distances of 20 m achieving a false rejection rate of less than 0.1 and a false detection rate of 0.01 (location noise σ = 5 m)". — [Jansen 2016 PDF](https://tippenhauer.de/publication/jansen-16-gps/jansen-16-gps.pdf)
- Kalantari & Larsson: Ashkan Kalantari, Erik G. Larsson, "Statistical test for GNSS spoofing attack detection by using multiple receivers on a rigid body," *EURASIP Journal on Advances in Signal Processing*, vol. 2020, no. 1, article 8, 26 Feb. 2020, DOI 10.1186/s13634-020-0663-z (open access; PDF https://asp-eurasipjournals.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s13634-020-0663-z). Model: N receivers at known relative positions p_n0 on a rigid 2-D platform with unknown translation b0 and rotation T(θ); I satellites; M averaged samples; Gaussian noise of known σ²; synchronised clocks; static during sampling. H0: `r[n,i] = |b0 + T p_n0 − s_i| + noise`; H1: `r[n,i] = |p_n0 + s'''_i| + τ_i + noise` (one spoofer per emulated satellite with forged position s'''_i and delay τ_i). GLRT with MLEs → compare difference of residual sums of squares under H0 and H1 to `(2σ²/M)·ln(γ_th)`; MLEs approximated by squared-range least squares (cyclic b0/θ optimisation, quartic in θ via Ferrari; τ_i closed form). Threshold: empirical (sweep γ_th, estimate Pfa from no-spoofer runs, Pd from spoofed runs). Attacker: *multiple* single-antenna spoofers, each emulating one satellite, higher power, knowing the receiver geometry, placed at least-squares-optimal positions for a chosen spoofed pose (b0_sp, T_sp). Results: detection improves with spoofed-pose distance (example 10³·[0.5,0.5] vs 10³·[5,5]), with spoofer mis-placement, with M, and "considerably" from 3 to 5 receivers; "increasing the number of GNSS receivers makes the attack easier to detect". Relative/absolute: uses known relative receiver geometry and *true satellite positions*; platform pose is estimated, not assumed. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13634-020-0663-z); [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1186/s13634-020-0663-z)
- Mykytyn et al.: Pavlo Mykytyn, Marcin Brzozowski, Zoya Dyka, Peter Langendoerfer, "GPS-Spoofing Attack Detection Mechanism for UAV Swarms," *2023 12th Mediterranean Conference on Embedded Computing (MECO)*, Budva, June 2023, pp. 1–8, DOI 10.1109/MECO58584.2023.10154998; author version arXiv:2301.12766. Statistic: ground station computes `d_GPS(UAV_1, UAV_i) = arccos(sin φ1 sin φi + cos φ1 cos φi cos Δλ)·R` (spherical law of cosines, Eq. 1; Pythagorean altitude correction) and compares with IR-UWB range d_UWB; alarm if `|d_GPS − d_UWB| > d_threshold`, with the threshold set from error budgets ("each GPS receiver's localization error is not larger than 4.9 meters … IR-UWB ranging error lays within 30 cm … the difference … should not exceed the sum of these two errors"; reducible with RTK, 1–3 cm). Single-transmitter case: "UAVs located in the affected area receive identical spoofed GPS signals and compute nearly the same localization result", so d_GPS ≈ 0 alone suffices; multi-transmitter case with different signals per UAV: still detected via the UWB comparison. No Pd/Pfa reported; a UWB range (≈ 150 m) is required. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.12766); [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/MECO58584.2023.10154998)
- Michieletto et al.: Giulia Michieletto, Francesco Formaggio, Angelo Cenedese, Stefano Tomasin, "Robust Localization for Secure Navigation of UAV Formations Under GNSS Spoofing Attack," *IEEE Transactions on Automation Science and Engineering*, vol. 20, no. 4, pp. 2383–2396, 2023 (early access 29 Sept. 2022), DOI 10.1109/TASE.2022.3208662. RigidShift characterises it as using "no independent absolute reference (RSS + GNSS/IMU only)" and "may therefore remain vulnerable to a sufficiently slow common-mode shift". Full text not consulted. — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109%2FTASE.2022.3208662); [RigidShift PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06885)
- Bi et al.: Siguo Bi, Kai Li, Shuyan Hu, Wei Ni, Cong Wang, Xin Wang, "Detection and Mitigation of Position Spoofing Attacks on Cooperative UAV Swarm Formations," arXiv:2312.03787 (Dec. 2023), stated as accepted by *IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security* (journal volume/pages/DOI unverified). Method: "formulate the problem of detecting malicious UAVs as a localization feasibility problem" relaxed to a semidefinite program on reported positions and inter-UAV distances; attack types "distributed, collusion, and mixed"; detection success rate improved by up to 65 % / 55 % / 51 % over benchmarks. RigidShift: "a uniform offset gives X = X̂, trivially feasible" (gauge-blind). — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03787); [RigidShift PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06885)
- GNSS-WASP (attack paper, directly relevant to coordinated attacker): Christopher Tibaldo, Harshad Sathaye, Giovanni Camurati, Srdjan Čapkun, "GNSS-WASP: GNSS Wide Area SPoofing," *Proceedings of the 34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security 2025)*, Seattle, Aug. 2025 (pages unverified; prepublication PDF https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity25/sec25cycle1-prepub-757-tibaldo.pdf). Countermeasure discussion: "Only relative errors are useful, as the defender does not know its absolute position"; proposed WASP-aware defences = 1 s rolling-window statistics, CUSUM, and widely spaced receiver grids (e.g. 2×2 grid with 100 m spacing, 6 edges) that accumulate the attack's deterministic approximation error Δd_i; also notes a victim can detect the attack by leaving the consistent 3-D area. — [USENIX PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity25/sec25cycle1-prepub-757-tibaldo.pdf)
- RigidShift (2026 preprint): Minseok Park, Joon Soo Yoo, "Rigid-Covert GNSS Spoofing of UAV Swarms: A Structural Blind Spot, Its Detection Limit, and Absolute-Anchor Defenses," arXiv:2608.06885 [cs.CR], 7 Aug 2026 (not peer-reviewed; all evaluations simulation-based). Model: `z_i(t) = x_i(t) + b_i(t) + n_i(t)`, n_i ~ N(0, σ_g² I); ranges `d_ij(t) = ||x_i − x_j|| + ε_ij`, ε_ij ~ N(0, σ_r²), over UWB. Prop. 1 (gauge freedom): "Any detector that is a function only of the relative quantities {z_i − z_j} and {d_ij} is invariant under z_i ↦ z_i + c for a common c ∈ R². Hence a common-mode b(t) is unobservable from relative channels alone." Prop. 2: ≥ 1 absolute anchor makes a common translation observable; ≥ 2 for rotation. Distance-verification baseline (their reproduction of Mykytyn et al.) scores `max_ij | ||z_i − z_j|| − d_ij |`, gated ≥ 3 consecutive frames, threshold at the α = 5 % false-alarm quantile of 120 no-attack runs. Table 6 (AUC, 200 seeds, covert ramp 2/5/20 cm/s): distance verification 0.56 / 0.49 / 0.48; cooperative SDP (Bi et al.) 0.54 / 0.49 / 0.48; over-determined geometry (anchor-aligned MDS) 0.84 / 1.00 / 1.00; absolute anchor 0.73 / 1.00 / 1.00; exact SDP flagged the covert ramp on only 1.7 % of runs. Attacker: "A single spoofer can preserve the formation exactly for N ≤ 9 [6], whereas preserving it at larger scale requires a wide-area multi-transmitter spoofer [38 = GNSS-WASP]"; detection floor for a calibrated anchor-residual detector `v* = 2γ/(1 − t_s/T)`. Also: "the multi-receiver countermeasure of [18 = Jansen 2016] exploits exactly this single-transmitter collapse". — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06885); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06885)
- Distributed multi-antenna attack with COTS hardware: Xiang Cheng, Hanchao Yang, Shinan Liu, Yaling Yang, "Distributed Multi-Antenna GPS Spoofing Attack using Off-the-Shelf Devices," *Proceedings of the 18th ACM Conference on Security and Privacy in Wireless and Mobile Networks (WiSec 2025)*, pp. 28–39, DOI 10.1145/3734477.3734706 (June 2025). Abstract: "spoofing signals must be aligned to within sub-50 ns for standard receivers to be fooled"; HackRF + Raspberry Pi prototype spoofed Android phones and commercial receivers. Relevance: practical realisation of the multi-antenna attacker that Jansen et al. (2016, Sect. 8) could not build. — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3734477.3734706); [HKU record](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/363051)

### Inferences
- Across the family, the pure position-domain tests (Tippenhauer 2011 countermeasure, Jansen 2016, Crowd-GPS-Sec Test 2, Mykytyn 2023, Bi 2023) are functions of {d_ij} or {z_i − z_j} only and thus share the null space {z_i ↦ R z_i + t}; the only works that break it do so by importing an absolute, non-GNSS reference (Crowd-GPS-Sec Test 1 via MLAT; RigidShift anchors), by exploiting the satellite geometry explicitly (Kalantari & Larsson use true s_i, so an attacker must also reproduce consistent satellite directions), or by exploiting residual geometric error of imperfect wide-area spoofers (GNSS-WASP's own countermeasures).
- Whether a "formation-preserving" attack is in the null space depends on who the paper assumes can build it: Tippenhauer 2011 / Jansen 2016 treat it as theoretically possible but practically infeasible; GNSS-WASP (2025) and Cheng et al. (2025) show hardware realisations; RigidShift (2026) treats it as the baseline threat.

| GNSS-WASP 2025 (attack) | — | Defender "does not know its absolute position … only relative errors are useful" | Yes: builds the formation-preserving multi-transmitter attack; detectability only via residual approximation error with large grids, windows, CUSUM — [USENIX PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity25/sec25cycle1-prepub-757-tibaldo.pdf) |
| RigidShift 2026 (preprint) | distance verification / SDP baselines vs anchor detectors | States Prop. 1: relative-only detectors invariant to common translation | Yes: central thesis; relative baselines at chance (AUC ≈ 0.5) — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06885) |

### Inferences
- The thesis claim "a rigid translation of the whole formation lies in the null space of purely relative tests" is (i) implicit in Tippenhauer et al.'s Result 7 (existence of the affine solution), (ii) acknowledged by Jansen et al. only through the infeasibility argument, (iii) demonstrated experimentally by GNSS-WASP on four COTS receivers, and (iv) stated as a formal proposition by Park & Yoo (2026). The strongest peer-reviewed support is GNSS-WASP (USENIX Security 2025); RigidShift is a non-peer-reviewed preprint and should be cited as such.

### Gaps
- No paper in the family was found that gives a closed-form Pd for a *formation-preserving* multi-transmitter attack against a relative-only test (all either assume it away or show AUC ≈ 0.5 empirically).
