\begin{abstract}
Multi-receiver spoofing detectors for UAV swarms assume that the spoofer
has a single transmission point, so that an attack collapses the
inter-UAV distances computed from GNSS. We study a distributed ground
spoofer that breaks this assumption: 24 fixed transmitters on a 10\,km
circle around the operating area, each replaying one satellite with the
authentic navigation message, simulated transmitter by transmitter with
three-dimensional propagation and a ground-to-UAV channel model. The
attack displaces a 25-UAV swarm by about 4\,km, but it does not preserve
the formation. Because ground transmitters cannot match the satellite
elevations, the computed formation is \emph{dilated}, by a median of
22\,\% of the baseline across missions, and a first-order least-squares
model reproduces this dilation epoch by epoch. Since a dilation increases the
inter-UAV distances, the collapse tests of Jansen et al.\ never fire, and
a worst-pair GNSS-vs-UWB check detects at most 26\,\% of the epochs at a
per-epoch false-alarm rate of $10^{-2}$ over formation spacings of
5--20\,m. A two-sided test on the median ratio between GNSS-derived and
UWB-measured distances detects the dilation in 38\,\% of the epochs at
5\,m and in 98\,\% at 20\,m. Raising the transmitters first shrinks the
dilation and then reverses it, with a zero crossing at about 7\,km of
altitude for a 10\,km array. Only transmitters placed on the satellites'
lines of sight, at several kilometres of slant range, are detected in
fewer than a quarter of the epochs.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
GNSS spoofing, UAV swarms, spoofing detection, multi-receiver, distributed
attack, relative distances, formation flight.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The open civilian GNSS signals carry no signal-level authentication.
Their structure and navigation message are public, and a transmitter
that reproduces them with enough power can make a receiver compute any
position and time of the attacker's choosing. Navigation-message
authentication, now operational on Galileo~\cite{euspa2025osnma}, does
not prevent this: an attacker that replays the authentic message and
forges only the signal delays, as in this paper, passes the check. Since
the first portable civilian spoofer was
demonstrated~\cite{humphreys2008assessing}, software-defined radios and
open-source signal generators have made the attack accessible for a few
hundred dollars, and incidents affecting ships, aircraft and unmanned
systems are reported routinely~\cite{psiaki2016gnss}. Unmanned aerial
vehicles (UAVs) are a natural target: they navigate on GNSS by default,
and their capture by spoofing has been demonstrated in the
field~\cite{kerns2014capture}. A swarm flying in formation, for
surveying, inspection, delivery or defense, multiplies both the value of
the target and the consequences of a successful hijack.

Among the countermeasures proposed in the last fifteen
years~\cite{psiaki2016gnss,altaweel2023fanets}, \emph{multi-receiver}
detection stands out for its simplicity and for the strength of its
guarantee. Its principle, established by Tippenhauer et
al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements}, is geometric. A spoofer with a
single antenna delivers the same signals to every receiver in its
coverage area, so all the victims compute the \emph{same} position fix.
A group of receivers that knows its own formation can therefore detect
the attack by checking that the mutual distances implied by the GNSS
fixes have not collapsed. Jansen et al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}
turned the check into a calibrated statistical test, showing that four
receivers a few metres apart reach a detection probability of $0.99$ at
a false-alarm rate of $10^{-5}$. Swaszek and
Hartnett~\cite{swaszek2013multicots} derived Neyman--Pearson optimal
tests for the same setting. For UAV swarms, the same idea has been
proposed with the formation measured on-line by inter-drone
ultra-wideband (UWB) ranging~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}, or verified as a
localization feasibility problem~\cite{bi2024sdp}. The guarantee rests
on one assumption: the attacker has a single transmission point.

This paper asks what happens when this assumption is dropped with a
\emph{physical} distributed spoofer rather than an idealized attacker
that imposes a common offset on every victim. The spoofer consists of
several fixed ground transmitters surrounding the swarm's operating
area, each impersonating one satellite, subject to the real propagation
geometry and to a ground-to-UAV channel. Formation-preserving spoofing
has been demonstrated in hardware when the transmitters are placed along
the satellite directions at altitude~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}. Ground
transmitters, however, cannot reproduce the satellites' elevation, and
neither the resulting deformation of the computed formation nor the
relative-distance tests able to observe it have been quantified.

We find that a ground array of two dozen fixed radios does not collapse
the formation but dilates it. The collapse tests
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} therefore never fire, and the
worst-pair GNSS-vs-UWB check of~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} misses most epochs
at standard GNSS noise. With the same UWB measurements
as~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}, a different statistic, a two-sided test on the
median distance ratio, recovers much of the detection. We study the
inter-UAV spacing of 5 to 20\,m typical of outdoor GNSS-based formation
flight (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a transmitter-by-transmitter simulation of a distributed ground
  spoofer attacking a moving UAV swarm, with per-epoch routing of
  satellites to transmitters, three-dimensional propagation of the
  replayed signals, and the elevation-dependent ground-to-UAV path-loss
  model of~\cite{alhourani2018cellular} (Section~\ref{sec:attack});
\item the finding that a ground array \emph{dilates} the computed
  formation, by a median of 22\,\% of the baseline across missions,
  because of the mismatch between satellite and transmitter elevations,
  together with a first-order least-squares model that predicts the
  dilation epoch by epoch (Sections~\ref{sec:residual}
  and~\ref{sec:validation});
\item a Monte Carlo evaluation, with mission-level bootstrap confidence
  intervals, of five relative-distance detectors: the three collapse
  variants of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, the worst-pair
  GNSS-vs-ranging check~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}, and a two-sided test on
  the median distance ratio. The dilation escapes the first four and is
  detected by the fifth in most epochs from 8\,m and reliably from 15\,m
  (Section~\ref{sec:detperf});
\item a transmitter-altitude sweep and a static line-of-sight
  (WASP-like) placement experiment that measure what it costs the
  attacker, in altitude, to remove the dilation: the signed dilation
  crosses zero at a transmitter elevation of about $36^\circ$ (7\,km of
  altitude for a 10\,km array), and a static constellation on the
  satellites' lines of sight at $\ge5$\,km slant range is detected in
  fewer than a quarter of the epochs (Section~\ref{sec:sweep}).
\end{itemize}
The simulator and the detection code are released with the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Multi-receiver detection in the position domain}
Tippenhauer et al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} formalized the GPS
group-spoofing problem and proved two complementary results. A
single-antenna spoofer places all victims at the same location
(Result~1), and a formation-preserving placement of one transmitter per
impersonated satellite always exists, along the satellite directions
(Result~7). Their countermeasure checks that the computed positions
preserve the physical formation, without a statistical model. Jansen et
al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} supplied the model: the pairwise
distances between receivers are Rician under authentic conditions and
Rayleigh under a single-transmitter attack, with spatially correlated
position noise ($\sigma\approx0.5$--$3$\,m, $\rho\approx0.4$--$0.6$
measured). Their test $f(\{d_{ij}\})<\lambda$ is evaluated in minimal,
maximal, majority and normalized-majority variants, the last being the
best. Their prototype with four receivers on a 5\,m square ran for three
hours without errors, and their own attempt at a distance-preserving
attack with four USRPs succeeded on one receiver and failed on two.
% CHECK: verify the four-USRP outcome against [jansen2016multireceiver].
Swaszek and Hartnett~\cite{swaszek2013multicots} derived Neyman--Pearson
optimal tests for COTS receivers at known relative positions, and later
fused GNSS fixes with inter-vehicle ranges for platoons under the
assumption that only one vehicle is spoofed~\cite{swaszek2017platoon}.
% CHECK: verify the "only one vehicle spoofed" assumption in [swaszek2017platoon].
Crowd-GPS-Sec~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec} applies the collapse
principle to crowdsourced air-traffic receivers, combined with an
absolute time-difference-of-arrival reference.

\subsection{Measurement-domain and swarm variants}
In the measurement domain, Stenberg et al.~\cite{stenberg2022multiple}
test double differences of pseudorange and carrier phase between
receivers with a $\chi^2$ GLRT, and find a minimum receiver separation
of about 5\,m with standard positioning. Kalantari and
Larsson~\cite{kalantari2020rigidbody} derive a GLRT against multiple
spoofers for receivers on a rigid body with unknown pose, and show that
more receivers make the attack easier to detect. Borio and
Gioia~\cite{borio2016sumofsquares} detect the single common direction of
arrival with a two-receiver carrier-phase sum-of-squares statistic. For
UAV swarms, Mykytyn et al.~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} compare GNSS-derived
inter-drone distances with UWB two-way ranging and raise an alarm when
they differ by more than about 5\,m; they consider a coordinated
per-drone attack impractical.
% CHECK: original wording was a direct quote ("virtually impossible");
% restore it only after verifying it verbatim in [mykytyn2023uwb].
Bi et al.~\cite{bi2024sdp} cast detection as a semidefinite
localization-feasibility problem that is robust to colluding members.
GNSS/INS fusion defenses, such as SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi}
and tightly coupled INS innovation
monitors~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor}, compare the
GNSS track with inertial dead reckoning and respond to the time
variation of the induced error.

\subsection{Coordinated and distributed spoofing}
Attack-side work has progressively approached the formation-preserving
placement of~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements}. We order it by
\emph{where the transmitters sit}, because this decides the residual
geometry.

\emph{Single transmitter.} Beyond Result~1
of~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements}, Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2024singlespoofer} analyse a single spoofer against several
receivers and show that preserving the formation \emph{exactly} is
possible only for a small group (at most about nine receivers); beyond
that, the formation is deformed and a relative-distance check detects
it. A follow-up~\cite{chen2025swarmspoofing} develops approximate
constructions that spoof more receivers at the price of a bounded error.
% CHECK: verify the "about nine receivers" bound in [chen2024singlespoofer].
A single transmitter therefore either collapses the swarm or scales
poorly, which motivates distributing the transmitters.

\emph{Ground arrays.} Ceccato et al.~\cite{ceccato2020spatial} designed
a multi-antenna ground spoofer with Wiener precoding that displaces a
swarm over a target plane while minimising a mean-squared position
error. It is a signal-processing model, not simulated on a flying swarm
and not confronted with a detector; GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}
lists the wrong angle of arrival of its ground-based signals as its main
limitation. Cheng et al.~\cite{cheng2025distributed} realised a
distributed multi-antenna spoofer with off-the-shelf radios, showed that
it requires sub-50\,ns alignment between transmitters, and steered
receivers in an anechoic chamber with an average \emph{position} error
of about 30\,m. Neither work studies what a ground array does to the
\emph{formation} of a swarm, nor whether a relative-distance detector
sees it.

\emph{Airborne constellations.} GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}
demonstrated in hardware a constellation of at least four synchronised
transmitters placed \emph{in line with the satellites at altitude}. It
spoofs receivers over a square-kilometre area independently of their
number, and preserves their relative distances with an average error of
about 1\,m in its reference configuration, bypassing
multi-receiver~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} and
sensor-fusion~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} countermeasures. Its authors
note that the residual bias of a real constellation decreases with the
transmitter altitude but cannot be eliminated short of placing the
transmitters in orbit. They also evaluate an attack-aware
countermeasure that flags an outlier among the pairwise distance
residuals, after a moving average or a CUSUM over time. Temporal
accumulation suits a residual whose sign varies from pair to pair. The
residual of a \emph{ground} array instead has a coherent sign (a
dilation), which allows aggregation \emph{over the pairs}
(Section~\ref{sec:principle}).

\emph{Idealised limit.} Park and Yoo~\cite{park2026rigidshift} state the
translation invariance of relative-only detectors as a formal
proposition, rooted in the gauge freedom of anchor-free network
localization~\cite{aspnes2006localization}, and evaluate it by
simulation with an idealised common offset rather than a physical
transmitter layout. Surveys of UAV spoofing
defenses~\cite{altaweel2023fanets,sebastian2026survey} note that
detectors relying on neighbours' positions fail when the neighbours are
inside the spoofed area, but do not quantify the effect for a physical
ground array.

\subsection{Positioning of this work}
The ground-array case is the one left uncharacterised: it has been
modelled~\cite{ceccato2020spatial} and built~\cite{cheng2025distributed},
but its effect on a formation and on relative-distance detectors has not
been measured. We study a ground array with one transmitter per
satellite, the canonical placement
of~\cite[Result~7]{tippenhauer2011requirements}, against a flying
swarm, and we quantify how far it is from the ideal formation-preserving
attacker of~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp,park2026rigidshift}.

\section{System and Distributed Spoofing Attack Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{UAV Swarm and Navigation Model}
We consider a swarm of $m=25$ UAVs flying at altitude $h$ in a square
$5\times5$ horizontal grid with spacing $g$ (the \emph{formation
spacing}) along a way-point trajectory (Table~\ref{tab:params}). The
swarm is served by a GNSS constellation of satellites in medium Earth
orbit. For UAV $u$ at position $\ptrue{u}(t)$ and satellite $s$ at
position $\mathbf{s}_s(t)$, visible when its elevation is positive, the
authentic propagation delay is
$\tau_{u,s}(t)=\lVert\mathbf{s}_s(t)-\ptrue{u}(t)\rVert/c$. Each receiver
has a constant clock bias $\delta t_u$ and independent additive white
Gaussian pseudorange noise of standard deviation
$\sigma_{\mathrm{pr}}=3$\,m, representative of standard (non-differential)
positioning.
% CHECK: elevation mask is 0 deg; real receivers typically use 5-15 deg.

Each UAV computes its position fix $\pest{u}$ independently from the
measured delays $\tilde{\tau}_{u,s}$ (authentic or spoofed) with the
standard iterated linearized least-squares (Gauss--Newton) solution over
the four unknowns $(x,y,z,\delta t)$; without attack and without noise
it recovers the true grid positions. In addition to GNSS, the UAVs
measure their mutual distances with an independent on-board ranging
sensor (UWB two-way ranging, as in~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}):
\begin{equation}
d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t) = \lVert\ptrue{i}(t)-\ptrue{j}(t)\rVert
 + \varepsilon_{ij}(t),\quad
\varepsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{rng}}),
\label{eq:uwb}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{\mathrm{rng}}=0.1$\,m and no ranging bias.

The UAVs follow their planned trajectory regardless of their GNSS fix;
the closed-loop reaction of guidance and formation control to the spoofed
fixes is not modelled (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Distributed Spoofing Attack}
\label{sec:attack}

The attacker moves the swarm's perceived position onto a fixed decoy
point, at about 4\,km from the true trajectory, while preserving the
perceived formation as far as its geometry allows. The attack is
simulated transmitter by transmitter, with no per-victim signal shaping.
As in~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}, the spoofer replays the authentic
navigation message, so the receivers use the true satellite ephemerides
and only the code phases are forged.

\emph{Transmitter geometry and per-epoch routing.}
$N_{\mathrm{sp}}=24$ fixed ground transmitters are placed on a circle of
radius $R_{\mathrm{sp}}=10$\,km that encloses the swarm's operating area
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}), each with an EIRP of 2\,dBm (1\,mW, 1\,dBi
antenna gain). Following the geometric argument
of~\cite[Result~7]{tippenhauer2011requirements}, each visible satellite
$s$ is routed, at every epoch, to the transmitter whose azimuth $\phi_k$,
as seen from the \emph{current} true swarm centre
$\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}(t)$, is closest to the satellite azimuth
$\phi_s$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sp}(s,t)=\arg\min_{k}\;
\bigl|\angle_{2\pi}\bigl(\phi_s(t)-\phi_k(t)\bigr)\bigr| ,
\label{eq:assign}
\end{equation}
where $\angle_{2\pi}(\cdot)$ wraps an angle to $(-\pi,\pi]$. With 24
transmitters the azimuth mismatch is at most $7.5^\circ$. The
transmitters never move; only the routing is updated, which requires the
attacker to track the true position of the swarm centre, the same
knowledge it needs to calibrate the replayed delays.

When a satellite is re-routed, the replayed delay changes abruptly for
every UAV except the centre, by the difference between the two
propagation residuals of~\eqref{eq:power}. The simulator applies no
smoothing, so this is the worst case for the attacker. Re-routing is
rare: at $g=10$\,m it affects 2.2\,\% of the satellite-epochs
(0.15 satellites per epoch on average), because consecutive
transmitters are $15^\circ$ apart in azimuth while the satellites move
slowly. Its effect on the computed formation is visible but
occasional: the per-epoch dilation changes by 0.5 percentage points
between consecutive epochs in the median, with a largest jump of 12.8
points over a mission. A real attacker could hand a satellite over
gradually, which would remove these transients.

\emph{Ground-to-UAV channel.}
The spoofing signals travel over a ground-to-air link, modelled with the
suburban cellular-to-UAV path loss of Al-Hourani and
Gomez~\cite{alhourani2018cellular}, which we also adopted in earlier
work~\cite{schilleci2025channel}. For a transmitter at horizontal
distance $d$ (in metres) from the UAV, with $\theta$ the elevation angle
of the UAV as seen from the transmitter (in degrees),
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PL}(d,\theta) = 10\,\alpha\log_{10}(d)
 + A\,(\theta-\theta_0)\,e^{-\frac{\theta-\theta_0}{B}} + \eta_0
 \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:alhourani}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=3.04$, $A=-23.29$, $B=4.14$, $\theta_0=-3.61^\circ$ and
$\eta_0=20.70$\,dB; shadowing is disabled for repeatability. The
excess-loss term is fitted in~\cite{alhourani2018cellular} for
$\theta\in[-2^\circ,10^\circ]$ and saturated at $10^\circ$ beyond. In
our deployment the largest angle met in any run is $6.0^\circ$, so the
model is always used within its validity range. The model was fitted on
cellular bands; we use it for the GNSS L1 band as a representative
ground-to-air loss.

\emph{Signal capture and replayed delay.}
For each UAV $u$ and visible satellite $s$, the receiver locks onto the
transmitter whenever the received spoofing power, computed
with~\eqref{eq:alhourani}, exceeds the authentic one. With the chosen
EIRP the spoofer margin over the satellite signal is between 13 and
29\,dB on every link (median 19.5\,dB), so capture is complete and
homogeneous across the swarm: no UAV mixes authentic and spoofed
satellites.

Transmitter $k=\mathrm{sp}(s,t)$ replays the signal of satellite $s$
with its code phase calibrated so that, at the true swarm centre, the
measured delay equals the decoy delay $\tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}$, i.e.\
the delay that satellite $s$ would exhibit at the decoy position. A UAV
at a different position receives the same transmission over a different
path and measures
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\tau}_{u,s} \;=\; \tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}\;-\;\delta t_u\;+\;
\frac{\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\ptrue{u}\rVert-\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}\rVert}{c},
\label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{q}_k$ is the transmitter position and both norms are true
three-dimensional slant distances. The last term is the propagation
residual: it vanishes for the centre UAV, which computes exactly the
decoy position, and is nonzero for the other UAVs. It does not depend on
the transmitted power, so once capture is complete the residual is
independent of it.

\subsection{Formation Deformation under Distributed Spoofing}
\label{sec:residual}
Write $\ptrue{u}=\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$ with
$\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}_u\rVert\ll\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}\rVert$,
and let $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_k$ be the unit vector from the centre to
transmitter $k$ and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s$ the unit line of sight from the
centre to satellite $s$. To first order, the residual delivered
by~\eqref{eq:power} is
$-(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_k\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)/c$. The delay that
would make UAV $u$ compute the decoy position \emph{rigidly displaced}
by $\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$ is instead
$-(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)/c$. The pseudorange
error with respect to the rigid attack, in metres, is therefore
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{u,s}\;=\;(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s-\hat{\mathbf{v}}_k)\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_u
\;\approx\;-(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_s\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)\,
\bigl(\cos\theta_k-\cos\mathrm{el}_s\bigr),
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{a}}_s$ is the horizontal unit vector in the satellite
azimuth, $\theta_k$ the elevation of transmitter $k$ as seen from the
swarm, and $\mathrm{el}_s$ the satellite elevation. The approximation
holds for a horizontal swarm and neglects the azimuth mismatch of the
routing~\eqref{eq:assign}; the exact first-order expression on the left
retains it.

For a ground array far from the swarm, $|\theta_k|$ is a few degrees, so
$\cos\theta_k\approx1>\cos\mathrm{el}_s$ and the coefficient
in~\eqref{eq:residual} is negative for every satellite. A UAV displaced
\emph{towards} the azimuth of satellite $s$ measures a pseudorange
\emph{shorter} than the rigid one, and a UAV displaced away from it a
longer one. The least-squares fix interprets shorter ranges to the
satellites ahead and longer ranges to those behind as a position farther
along $\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$. Every UAV is thus pushed outward along
its own displacement from the centre, and the computed formation is
\emph{dilated}. The coefficient vanishes only when
$|\cos\theta_k|=\cos\mathrm{el}_s$, i.e.\ for satellites near the
horizon or when $|\theta_k|$ approaches $\mathrm{el}_s$. Since the cosine
is even, this happens either for transmitters raised in altitude
(Section~\ref{sec:sweep}) or, symmetrically, for transmitters seen at a
comparable \emph{depression} angle, i.e.\ close to the swarm and well
below it. We verified the second branch by placing the array 1\,km below
the swarm: at horizontal ranges of 5, 2 and 1\,km the median transmitter
elevation is $-14.7^\circ$, $-34.9^\circ$ and $-58.1^\circ$, and the
signed dilation is $+22.0$\,\%, $+5.4$\,\% and $-22.7$\,\%. It crosses
zero near $|\theta_k|=35^\circ$, where $\cos\theta_k$ matches the mean
$\cos\mathrm{el}_s$ of the constellation, as~\eqref{eq:residual}
predicts. A low array very close to the swarm is therefore an
alternative to altitude, at the cost of a coverage area of the same size
as the stand-off distance.

\emph{Predicted dilation.} Let $\mathbf{G}$ be the geometry matrix of
the four-unknown fix, with rows $[-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s^{\mathsf T},\,1]$,
and $\mathbf{B}$ the matrix with rows
$(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s-\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sp}(s,t)})^{\mathsf T}$,
so that the error vector of~\eqref{eq:residual} is
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_u=\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$. The
linearized least-squares response to this error gives the computed
position
\begin{equation}
\pest{u}=\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{dec}}+(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{M})\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_u,
\qquad
\mathbf{M}=\bigl[(\mathbf{G}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{G})^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{B}\bigr]_{1:3},
\label{eq:lsmodel}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]_{1:3}$ keeps the three position rows and discards the
clock row. The predicted computed distance of each pair is
$\lVert(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{M})(\boldsymbol{\delta}_i-\boldsymbol{\delta}_j)\rVert$,
including the vertical component of the error. We refer
to~\eqref{eq:lsmodel} as the \emph{first-order model}.
A cruder estimate follows by matching~\eqref{eq:residual}, for a single
satellite, with the position response
$-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s\!\cdot\!\Delta\mathbf{p}$ under the assumption
$\Delta\mathbf{p}\parallel\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$, which gives
$\Delta\mathbf{p}=(1/\cos\mathrm{el}_s-1)\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$.
Averaged over the constellation, this yields the dilation scale
\begin{equation}
\kappa_0\;=\;\frac{1}{\langle\cos\mathrm{el}_s\rangle}-1 ,
\label{eq:dilation}
\end{equation}
which is 0.3--0.4 for typical elevation distributions. In our runs
$\kappa_0$ overestimates the dilation, because the clock and vertical
unknowns of the fix absorb part of the error
(Section~\ref{sec:validation}); it is useful only as an order of
magnitude. Both~\eqref{eq:lsmodel} and~\eqref{eq:dilation} are
independent of the transmitter power and of the formation spacing $g$:
the dilation is a fixed fraction of the baseline.

\section{Relative-Distance Spoofing Detection}
\label{sec:detection}

\subsection{Detection Principle}
\label{sec:principle}
A defender compares the inter-UAV distances implied by the GNSS fixes
with the independent UWB ranges through the normalized ratios
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}(t)=\frac{\hat{d}_{ij}(t)}{d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t)},
\qquad
\hat{d}_{ij}=\lVert\pest{i}-\pest{j}\rVert ,
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
computed over all $\binom{m}{2}=300$ pairs at every epoch, with
$d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}$ the UWB measurement~\eqref{eq:uwb}. Under
authentic conditions $r_{ij}\approx1$.

The \emph{sign} of the distortion distinguishes the two attack regimes.
A single-transmitter spoofer collapses the fixes onto one
point~\cite[Result~1]{tippenhauer2011requirements}, so the classical
detectors threshold a statistic that \emph{decreases} when the pairwise
distances shrink. A ground array with one transmitter per satellite
dilates the formation (Section~\ref{sec:residual}), so the same
statistic \emph{increases}, into the tail that a one-sided collapse test
does not examine. Because the dilation has the same sign on all pairs,
it can be detected by aggregating over the pairs at a single epoch, with
no need for temporal accumulation.

\subsection{Detection Statistics}
\emph{Collapse tests (Jansen et al.).}
The detector of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} declares spoofing when the
pairwise distances \emph{shrink}:
\begin{equation}
\text{alarm if } f\bigl(\{r_{ij}\}\bigr)<\lambda ,
\label{eq:jansen}
\end{equation}
with the three variants of its Appendix~A:
\begin{align}
f_{\min}  &= \min_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\max}  &= \max_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\mathrm{maj}} &= \operatorname*{med}_{i<j} r_{ij}.
\label{eq:variants}
\end{align}
$f_{\min}$ alarms if any pair has shrunk, $f_{\max}$ only if all pairs
have, and $f_{\mathrm{maj}}$ if at least half of the pairs have; the
latter is equivalent to the normalized majority vote
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, since at least half of the normalized
distances are below $\lambda$ if and only if their median is. Jansen et
al.\ set $\lambda$ from a Rician ($H_0$) / Rayleigh ($H_1$) model of the
pairwise distance with spatially correlated position noise; we calibrate
$\lambda$ empirically (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\emph{Two-sided tests.}
These fire on a deviation of the distance ratios from one in either
direction. The \emph{worst-pair} test
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{wp}} = \max_{i<j}\,\bigl|\,r_{ij}-1\,\bigr|,
\qquad \text{alarm if } T_{\mathrm{wp}}>\lambda ,
\label{eq:cons}
\end{equation}
is the ratio form of the GNSS-vs-UWB check of~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb},
which alarms if $|d_{\mathrm{GPS}}-d_{\mathrm{UWB}}|>d_{\mathrm{thr}}$
on any pair, and the per-epoch core of the outlier test evaluated
in~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}. Because it is governed by the noisiest
pair, it is weak against a bias shared by all pairs. Against such a
bias an \emph{aggregate} statistic is more powerful, such as the
$\chi^2$ sum over double differences of~\cite{stenberg2022multiple} or
simply the median. We therefore also evaluate the \emph{two-sided
majority} test
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{maj}} = \bigl|\operatorname*{med}_{i<j} r_{ij}-1\bigr|,
\qquad \text{alarm if } T_{\mathrm{maj}}>\lambda ,
\label{eq:maj2}
\end{equation}
the two-sided counterpart of $f_{\mathrm{maj}}$. We centre both
two-sided statistics on one for comparability with~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb};
the consequence of the noise bias of the median ratio is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:sweep}. Finally, since the dilation is persistent, both
two-sided statistics are also evaluated after a moving average over 12
epochs (1\,min) within each mission.

\subsection{Threshold Selection and Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Following the Neyman--Pearson approach
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver,kay1998detection}, thresholds are
calibrated on the $H_0$ (no-spoofing) scenario simulated with the same
noise levels: $\lambda$ is the empirical $\PFA$-quantile of the
statistic over all $H_0$ epochs (lower quantile for the collapse tests,
upper for the two-sided tests), with a per-epoch $\PFA=10^{-2}$. This
rate is higher than the $10^{-5}$ targeted
in~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, because lower rates cannot be
estimated reliably from the $4800$ $H_0$ epochs available per spacing.

We report (i) the detection probability $\PD$, the fraction of attacked
epochs that raise an alarm, and (ii) the receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve, the locus of $(\PFA,\PD)$ pairs as
$\lambda$ is swept, with its area (AUC)~\cite{kay1998detection}. The
AUC is the probability that the statistic of a random attacked epoch
falls on the alarm side of that of a random $H_0$ epoch. An AUC of $0.5$
is chance; an AUC near $1$ means that the two distributions are
separated on the side the test examines; an AUC near $0$ means that
they are separated on the \emph{opposite} side, i.e.\ the attack is
observable, but only in the tail that the test does not examine.

\subsection{Simulation Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Table~\ref{tab:params} summarizes the parameters. We sweep the formation
spacing over $g\in\{5,8,10,12,15,20\}$\,m, the range of outdoor
formation flight. Experimental leader--follower formations of
quadcopters have been flown at 5\,m spacing with separation errors of
0.4\,m~\cite{cheok2022formation}; a design method that derives the
minimum safe spacing from the combined navigation and control error
yields spacings of a few metres for GNSS-based
navigation~\cite{guo2025separation}; the multi-receiver prototype
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} uses a 5\,m square, and the swarm
detector of~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} is designed around a 5\,m GPS error
budget. Spacings below a few metres, as in drone light shows, require
centimetre-level RTK positioning and are outside the standard-GNSS
regime considered here; wider spacings are typical of coverage and
surveillance swarms rather than of formation flight.

For each spacing we simulate $N_{\mathrm{MC}}=20$ independent missions
(random start time, clock biases and noise), each of 240 epochs, with
and without the attack. The simulator and the detection code are

Because the epochs of one mission share clocks, trajectory and satellite
geometry, the Monte Carlo unit is the \emph{mission}: 95\,\% confidence
intervals on $\PD$ are obtained by a paired bootstrap over missions (500
resamples; the $H_0$ and attacked runs of a mission share the random
seed, and the threshold is re-calibrated in every resample).

Besides the detection metrics, we report two geometric quantities. The
\emph{distortion}
\begin{equation}
E(t)=\operatorname*{med}_{i<j}\frac{|\hat{d}_{ij}(t)-d_{ij}(t)|}{d_{ij}(t)}
\label{eq:E}
\end{equation}
is the unsigned evidence available to any relative-distance detector; it
includes the noise. The \emph{signed dilation}
\begin{equation}
D=\operatorname*{med}_{t}\;\operatorname*{med}_{i<j}\frac{\hat{d}_{ij}(t)-d_{ij}(t)}{d_{ij}(t)}
\label{eq:D}
\end{equation}
is measured on noiseless runs of a mission. It is positive for a
dilation and negative for a contraction, and it isolates the effect of
the attack geometry.

\subsection{Formation Distortion under Attack}
\label{sec:distortion}
The attack succeeds: every UAV is captured on all its visible
satellites, and the computed position of the centre UAV coincides with
the decoy at every epoch. Since the decoy is fixed and the swarm moves,
the displacement varies along the mission, from about 1 to 6\,km
(4.0\,km on average; Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}, top). The bottom panel
shows the price paid by a ground array: the distortion $E$ stays above
the no-attack noise floor throughout the mission.

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_timeseries}

The dilation $D$ has a median of $+22$\,\% over the 20 missions at
$g=10$\,m (noiseless), but varies widely between missions, from $6.6$
to $84$\,\% (10th--90th percentiles), depending on the elevations of the
satellites in view. Over the formation spacings, $E$ under $H_0$ is pure
GNSS noise and decays as $1/g$, from 22\,\% at $g=5$\,m to 4.6\,\% at
20\,m, while under attack it decreases only from 38\,\% to 23\,\%,
approaching the scale-free dilation as the noise contribution fades. The
distortion
is a dilation, not a collapse: the median distance ratio
$\operatorname{med}r_{ij}$ is $1.23$ under attack at $g=20$\,m against
$1.01$ without attack ($1.37$ against $1.16$ at 5\,m, where noise biases
both upward), and it exceeds one in every attacked epoch.

\subsection{Validation of the First-Order Model}
\label{sec:validation}
Figure~\ref{fig:validation} compares the first-order
model~\eqref{eq:lsmodel} with the full nonlinear simulation on a
noiseless attacked mission at $g=20$\,m. The model predicts a median
distortion of $24.4$\,\% against $24.5$\,\% measured, with correlation
$1.00$ and a largest deviation of $0.4$ percentage points, so the
linearization holds at these baselines. The comparison of three
predictions shows where the dilation comes from. The crude
scale~\eqref{eq:dilation} gives $34.5$\,\%, the horizontal part of the
model $18.8$\,\%, and the full model $24.4$\,\%. The clock and vertical
unknowns of the fix absorb part of the pseudorange error (hence
$34.5\to24.4$), while the vertical component of the position error, an
altitude tilt proportional to $\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$, contributes about
a quarter of the three-dimensional distortion (hence $18.8\to24.4$). The
dependence of $\mathbf{M}$ on the satellites in view also explains the
spread of the dilation across missions.

\emph{Collapse tests.} As expected from the sign of the distortion, the
three Jansen variants never detect the dilation: $\PD\le0.011$ (95\,\%
upper bound) at every spacing, at or below the false-alarm rate. Their
ROC area is \emph{below} chance, falling from $0.31$, $0.43$ and $0.17$
at $g=5$\,m to $0.12$, $0.26$ and $0.001$ at 20\,m for the minimal,
maximal and majority variants. This does not mean that the attack is
invisible: the two distributions are separable, but on the side that a
collapse test does not examine. The majority variant, the one
recommended in~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, reports exactly zero at
every spacing, and no choice of threshold changes this: its statistic
under attack has a minimum of $1.04$ over the 4800 attacked epochs,
while the $H_0$ threshold that yields $\PFA=10^{-2}$ is $1.03$ at 5\,m
and $0.98$ at 10--20\,m, so the attacked statistic never enters the
alarm region.

\emph{Worst-pair test.} The worst-pair test is masked by noise: $\PD$
rises only from $0.03$ at 5\,m to $0.26$ at 20\,m (ROC area $0.57$ to
$0.74$),
with wide confidence intervals that reflect the variability of the
dilation across missions. The statistic~\eqref{eq:cons} is governed by
the noisiest of 300 pairs, and at $\sigma_{\mathrm{pr}}=3$\,m the $H_0$
tail of that pair is wider than the dilation of a typical pair over the
whole spacing range. A one-minute moving average raises $\PD$ only to
$0.12$--$0.38$ (Table~\ref{tab:window}).

\emph{Two-sided majority test.} The aggregate statistic~\eqref{eq:maj2}
averages the noise over all pairs and retains the common bias, which is
the signature of a ground array. It detects the dilation in $0.38$ of
the epochs at 5\,m, $0.57$--$0.71$ at 8--12\,m, and reliably from 15\,m
($0.85$ at 15\,m, $0.98$ at 20\,m), with ROC area from $0.83$ at 5\,m to
$0.999$ at 20\,m. At
10\,m its ROC (Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}) mirrors that of the collapse majority
test: the same statistic, tested in the other tail. With one-minute
integration it reaches $\PD=0.52$ at 5\,m, $0.80$ at 10\,m, $0.93$ at
12\,m and $1.00$ from 15\,m (Table~\ref{tab:window}); these values are
optimistic, because our noise is white in time
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\emph{Wider spacings.} The dilation is a fixed fraction of the baseline,
whereas the noise floor decays as $1/g$, so wider formations or
lower-noise fixes (RTK) favour the two-sided tests. In supplementary
runs at 50 and 100\,m spacing (20 missions each, same attack model and
seeds), the two-sided majority test reaches $\PD=1.00$, the worst-pair
test $0.56$ and $0.65$ (AUC $0.86$ and $0.96$), and the collapse
detectors remain at zero.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Attack Geometry}
\label{sec:sweep}
\emph{Array enclosure.} The attack requires an array that encloses the
operating area, and what fails outside it is the azimuth routing, not
the elevation mismatch. With a 2.5\,km array centred on the start point
the swarm leaves the circle for 75\,\% of the mission. While it is
inside, the routing matches the satellite azimuths to within
$4.6^\circ$ (median) and the signed dilation is $+24.9$\,\%, the same
regime as the enclosing 10\,km array ($+22$\,\%), as expected
from~\eqref{eq:residual}, which does not depend on the stand-off
distance. Once the swarm is outside, every transmitter lies in a narrow
angular sector behind it, the median azimuth mismatch of the routing
rises to $83.5^\circ$, and the formation is no longer dilated but
strongly contracted ($-46$\,\%), which a two-sided test detects
immediately. The rest of this subsection uses the enclosing 10\,km array
and varies the transmitter altitude.

\emph{Transmitter altitude.}
By~\eqref{eq:residual}, the dilation decreases as the transmitters are
raised towards the satellite elevations, and it vanishes for every
satellite only if each transmitter lies on its satellite's line of sight,
the placement realized at altitude by
GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}. We quantify this trade-off at
$g=10$\,m, the centre of the spacing range, on the same 20 missions
(same seeds) as the main results. The dilation $D$ is measured on
noiseless runs; the noisy runs serve only to measure $\PD$. Elevated
transmitters use a free-space link instead of the ground channel
of~\eqref{eq:alhourani}, with the EIRP raised to 10\,mW. Capture is
complete in every mission (minimum margin 13.9\,dB) and, since the
delays do not depend on the channel, the ground point of the sweep
coincides with the main results.

The 24-transmitter, 10\,km array is raised from the ground to 10\,km,
i.e.\ from $\theta_k\approx0^\circ$ to $47^\circ$ as seen from the swarm
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}). The first-order model~\eqref{eq:lsmodel}
reproduces the measured distortion at every altitude (top panel). The
median signed dilation decreases monotonically from $+22$\,\% on the
ground to $+15.5$\,\% at $19^\circ$ and $+8.8$\,\% at $27^\circ$,
crosses zero at $\theta_k\approx36^\circ$ (7\,km of altitude for this
array), and becomes a \emph{contraction} of $-15$\,\% at $47^\circ$.
With a single altitude for all transmitters the residual cannot vanish
for every satellite at once: satellites below $\theta_k$ pull the
formation inward, while those above still push it outward. The spread
across missions is large; at the crossing the 10th--90th percentiles of
$D$ are $-15$\,\% and $+62$\,\%, depending on the elevations of the
satellites in view. For this reason the distortion $E$ pooled over
missions is smallest ($10.7$\,\%) before the crossing, at $27^\circ$,
and is $14.6$\,\% at the crossing itself.

The detectors follow the sign of the residual (middle panel). The
two-sided majority test decreases from $\PD=0.63$ on the ground to a
minimum of $0.39$ at the crossing and rises again to $0.59$ at
$47^\circ$ ($0.80$, $0.53$ and $0.71$ with one-minute integration). The
collapse test, blind on the ground, starts firing as the formation
contracts ($\PD=0.22$ at $27^\circ$, $0.44$ at the crossing, $0.64$ at
$47^\circ$). The worst-pair tests stay at $0.05$--$0.18$ throughout,
because the residual that survives at the crossing, 10--15\,\% of the
baseline with a sign that varies across pairs and missions, remains
below their noise. The minimum of the aggregate test is not lower
because the crossing altitude depends on the mission: at any single
altitude, most missions are still dilated or already contracted.

The bottom panel makes this explicit by plotting $\PD$ of every mission
and configuration against its dilation $D$. The two-sided majority test
is blind for $D\in[-8,+5]\,\%$ ($\PD<0.1$) and detects outside this
window ($0.71$ for $D$ between $+12$ and $+20$\,\%, $0.93$ beyond
$+20$\,\%, $0.81$ beyond $-20$\,\%); the collapse test is its mirror
image on the contraction side. The window is shifted towards contraction
by the noise bias of the median ratio. Under $H_0$ the median ratio is
$1+b_0$, with $b_0\approx0.045$ at 10\,m because noise lengthens
distances on average; under attack it is approximately $1+D+b_0$. The
attacked statistic $|D+b_0|$ thus falls within the $H_0$ spread for $D$
slightly negative, and a contraction of a few percent reads as no
attack. Centring the test on the calibrated $H_0$ median $1+b_0$ instead
of one is expected to re-centre the window on $D=0$.

\emph{static} version of the GNSS-WASP geometry: one transmitter per
satellite visible at the start of the mission, placed on its line of
sight from the array centre at slant range $R_{\mathrm{los}}$ (altitude
$R_{\mathrm{los}}\sin\mathrm{el}_s$, up to 9\,km for
$R_{\mathrm{los}}=10$\,km). The constellation is built once, from the
satellites visible at the first epoch; a satellite that rises later is
routed by~\eqref{eq:assign} to the transmitter closest in azimuth, which
sits on another satellite's line of sight, so it is spoofed but with a
larger residual. Such a constellation is on the lines of sight
only for a swarm near the reference point, as in GNSS-WASP, whose bias
grows with the distance from it. The experiment therefore uses a
loitering mission confined within 700\,m of the centre, and compares it
with the ground circle on the same mission (Table~\ref{tab:los}). The
line-of-sight placement cancels the signed dilation ($+20.5$\,\% for the
ground circle; $+5.0$, $+0.9$ and $+0.3$\,\% at $R_{\mathrm{los}}=2$, 5
and 10\,km) but leaves a residual distortion of 11, 5.8 and 4.3\,\% of
varying sign, because the transmitters are fixed while the satellites
move. The two-sided majority test drops from $\PD=0.62$ (AUC $0.96$) to
$0.29$, $0.23$ and $0.18$ (AUC $0.75$, $0.65$ and $0.60$), the
worst-pair test to $0.04$, and the collapse test fires in about a tenth
of the epochs on the contracting missions. GNSS-WASP's transmitters
track the satellites, so this static residual is an upper bound on the
WASP error, and its detectability an upper bound on WASP's. A
constellation on the lines of sight at slant ranges of 5\,km and beyond
is detected in fewer than a quarter of the epochs by the best
relative-distance test at this spacing, against about two thirds for the
ground array, at the price of airborne platforms several kilometres high.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsubsection*{What a ground array can and cannot do}
A distributed ground spoofer defeats the premise of the classical
multi-receiver countermeasure, since it does not collapse the formation,
but it cannot preserve the formation either: the elevation mismatch
of~\eqref{eq:residual} dilates it by a median of 22\,\% of the baseline,
independently of the transmitter power and of the formation spacing. For
the defender this is a change of signature, not a loss of
observability. Collapse tests should be replaced, or at least
complemented, by two-sided aggregate tests on the distance ratios. In
our simulation these detect the dilation reliably at 15--20\,m and in a
majority of epochs at 8--12\,m with standard positioning, and reliably
from 12--15\,m with one minute of integration. Raising the transmitters
reduces and then reverses the dilation, so a defender should test the
distance ratios on \emph{both} sides and expect a blind window of a few
percent of dilation around zero. A spoofer that compensates the residual
with per-satellite altitudes is left to future work.

\subsubsection*{Noise model and operating point}
Our pseudorange noise is independent across receivers and white in
time. In practice, a large part of the GNSS error (ionosphere,
troposphere, ephemeris) is common to receivers a few metres apart, with
correlation coefficients of 0.4--0.6 measured
in~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, and it is correlated over minutes.
Spatial correlation lowers the noise floor of every relative-distance
test, including the worst-pair test, so our worst-pair results are
pessimistic. Temporal correlation reduces the gain of a moving average,
so our one-minute integration results (Table~\ref{tab:window}) are
optimistic. The per-epoch false-alarm rate of $10^{-2}$ corresponds to
about two false alarms per 20-minute mission; an operational detector
would need a mission-level false-alarm constraint, e.g.\ with a
sequential test. Finally, the UWB ranges are modelled as unbiased. A
calibration or non-line-of-sight bias of a few decimetres is a few
percent of a 5--10\,m baseline, of the same order as the blind window of
the two-sided majority test, and would have to be calibrated out.

\subsubsection*{Scope of the result}
The evaluation covers detectors that are functions of relative
positions or distances, over the spacings of GNSS-based formation
flight. It does \emph{not} extend to (i) \emph{direction-of-arrival}
detectors: a circle of ground transmitters produces arrivals at
near-zero elevation, inconsistent with the satellite constellation, so
multi-antenna arrays with attitude
information~\cite{psiaki2016gnss,borio2016sumofsquares} would detect our
attacker unless its transmitters are placed along the satellite
directions as in~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}; (ii)
\emph{absolute-reference} checks, such as ranging to fixed
infrastructure, TDoA from ground sensor
networks~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec}, or terrain and
signal-of-opportunity positioning, which directly observe a displacement
of several kilometres; (iii) GNSS/INS detectors such as
SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} and INS innovation
monitors~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor}, which respond to
the time variation of the induced error and were not evaluated.

\subsubsection*{Relation to formation-preserving attacks}
Tippenhauer et al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} prove that a
formation-preserving placement exists,
GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp} approximates it with transmitters
in line with the satellites at altitude, and Park and
Yoo~\cite{park2026rigidshift} show that an exactly common translation is
invisible to any relative-only detector. Our attacker is more
constrained, and the dilation we measure and model is what separates it
from those ideal attacks. Section~\ref{sec:sweep} connects the two
regimes: raising a common-altitude array changes the sign of the
residual rather than removing it, and only a constellation on the lines
of sight at several kilometres of slant range approaches the regime
of~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}. Even then, a static constellation leaves
a 4--6\,\% residual over a 20-minute mission, because the satellites
move while the transmitters do not.

\subsubsection*{Other simplifications}
The result assumes an array that encloses the swarm's operating area; a
static array that the swarm leaves deforms the formation by more than
50\,\% and is readily detected. The decoy position is fixed and the
attack is modelled in its steady state after capture; the capture
transient, time-varying decoys and the routing switches
of~\eqref{eq:assign} are not modelled. The UAVs fly open loop: in a real
swarm, guidance would follow the spoofed position, and a GNSS-based
formation controller would react to the computed dilation by contracting
the true formation. The attacker is assumed to know the true position of
the swarm centre at every epoch. The simulator uses a spherical Earth, a
synthetic single-constellation geometry with a $0^\circ$ elevation mask,
no multipath or shadowing, and signal capture by power comparison
without tracking-loop dynamics. We did not vary the swarm size $m$ or
the number of transmitters $N_{\mathrm{sp}}$; with few UAVs, the median
over pairs averages less noise and the two-sided majority test loses
power. Twenty missions per spacing bound $\PD$ to within about $\pm0.1$
for the two-sided tests; the conclusions on the collapse detectors do not
depend on this resolution.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We simulated a distributed ground spoofer, with 24 fixed transmitters,
one impersonated satellite each, three-dimensional propagation and a
ground-to-UAV channel, attacking a UAV swarm at the 5--20\,m spacings of
outdoor GNSS-based formation flight, and evaluated relative-distance
detectors against it. The attack displaces the swarm by about 4\,km but
cannot preserve its formation: the mismatch between satellite and
transmitter elevations dilates the computed formation by a median of
22\,\% of the baseline, which a first-order least-squares model
reproduces epoch by epoch. The dilation lies in the tail that the
collapse detectors of Jansen et al.\ do not test ($\PD\le0.011$, AUC
down to $0.001$), and noise largely masks it for the worst-pair
GNSS-vs-UWB check ($\PD\le0.26$). A two-sided test on the median
distance ratio detects it in a majority of epochs from 8\,m and reliably
from 15\,m ($\PD$ from $0.38$ at 5\,m to $0.98$ at 20\,m). Raising the
transmitters does not remove the residual but reverses it. The
attacker's best common altitude is a narrow, mission-dependent band
around the zero crossing (about 7\,km for a 10\,km array), and only a
constellation on the satellites' lines of sight at several kilometres
of slant range comes close to undetectability. Relative-distance
detection against distributed ground spoofers should therefore test the
distance ratios for deviations on both sides rather than for collapse,
and should be complemented by direction-of-arrival or absolute-reference
information against airborne attackers that can also reproduce the
elevations.
