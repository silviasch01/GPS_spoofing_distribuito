\begin{abstract}
Multi-receiver spoofing detection is regarded as one of the most robust
countermeasures against civilian GNSS spoofing: receivers in a known
formation compare the mutual distances implied by their GNSS fixes with
the formation geometry, and a single-transmitter spoofer---which forces
every victim onto the same position---is detected with near-certain
probability. We evaluate this detector family against a \emph{physical
distributed} spoofer: 24 fixed ground transmitters deployed around the
operating area of a 25-UAV swarm, each replaying the satellite closest to
it in azimuth, with capture decided by received power through an
elevation-dependent ground-to-UAV channel and with the replayed code
phase propagating over the true three-dimensional path to every drone.
No per-victim pre-compensation and no idealized attack are simulated. We
implement the collapse detector of Jansen et al.\ (ACSAC~2016) in its
three variants and a two-sided formation-consistency test representative
of GNSS-vs-ranging checks, and evaluate them over 20 Monte Carlo missions
per configuration with bootstrap confidence intervals. The physical
attack displaces the swarm by 4\,km while all three collapse detectors
stay at $\PD\le0.011$ (95\,\% upper bound) at every formation spacing
from 5 to 100\,m: a distributed spoofer does not collapse the formation,
so the signature these detectors look for never appears. The consistency
test retains only partial power, from $\PD=0.03$ at 5\,m to $0.65$ at
100\,m at $\PFA=10^{-2}$, because the residual non-rigidity of ground
transmitters (about 22\,\% of the baseline, caused by the mismatch between
satellite and transmitter elevations) is masked by GNSS noise for compact
swarms. We quantify this residual, show that it depends critically on
deploying the array around the operating area, and delimit the result
with respect to direction-of-arrival and absolute-reference defenses.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
GNSS spoofing, UAV swarms, spoofing detection, multi-receiver, distributed

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% TODO (autore): riscrivere/estendere l'introduzione. Questa e' una bozza
%% minima per rendere il documento compilabile e coerente.

Civilian GNSS signals are unauthenticated and can be forged by
commodity software-defined radios~\cite{humphreys2008assessing}. Among
the proposed countermeasures, \emph{multi-receiver} detection stands out
for its simplicity: since a single-antenna spoofer forces every victim in
its coverage area to compute the same position
fix~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements}, a group of receivers that knows
its own formation can detect the attack by checking that the mutual
distances implied by the GNSS fixes have not
collapsed~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver,swaszek2013multicots}. The same
principle underpins recent proposals for UAV swarms, which compare
GNSS-derived inter-drone distances against independent ultra-wideband
(UWB) ranging~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb,bi2024sdp}.

This paper asks whether the guarantee survives a \emph{distributed}
attacker, i.e.\ several fixed ground transmitters surrounding the
operating area. Our contributions are:
(i) a physical simulation model of a distributed ground spoofer attacking
a moving UAV swarm, with per-epoch satellite-to-transmitter routing,
true three-dimensional propagation of the replayed signals, and an
elevation-dependent ground-to-UAV channel~\cite{alhourani2018cellular};
(ii) a faithful implementation of the collapse detector of Jansen et
al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} and of a two-sided formation
consistency test~\cite{stenberg2022multiple,mykytyn2023uwb};
(iii) a Monte Carlo evaluation with confidence intervals showing that the
physical attack defeats the collapse detectors entirely and the
consistency test for compact swarms; and
(iv) an analysis of the residual non-rigidity of ground transmitters and
of its dependence on the array deployment, together with an honest
delimitation of the result with respect to direction-of-arrival and
absolute-reference defenses.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% TODO (autore): riscrivere. Bozza strutturata con le citazioni chiave.

\subsubsection*{Multi-receiver position-domain detection}
Tippenhauer et al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} formalized the GPS
group-spoofing problem and proposed checking that the victims' computed
positions preserve their physical formation; Jansen et
al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} turned the check into a statistical
test on pairwise distances with Rician/Rayleigh error models and
spatially correlated noise, and Swaszek and
Hartnett~\cite{swaszek2013multicots} derived Neyman--Pearson optimal
variants for COTS receivers. Crowd-GPS-Sec~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec}
applies the same collapse principle to crowdsourced air-traffic
receivers.

\subsubsection*{Measurement-domain and swarm variants}
Stenberg et al.~\cite{stenberg2022multiple} test double differences of
pseudorange and carrier phase across receivers ($\chi^2$/GLRT), Kalantari
and Larsson~\cite{kalantari2020rigidbody} derive a GLRT for receivers on
a rigid body with unknown pose explicitly against multiple spoofers, and
Borio and Gioia~\cite{borio2016sumofsquares} detect the single common
direction of arrival with a two-receiver sum-of-squares statistic. For
UAV swarms, Mykytyn et al.~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} compare GNSS distances
with UWB two-way ranging, and Bi et al.~\cite{bi2024sdp} cast detection
as a semidefinite localization-feasibility problem. GNSS/INS fusion
defenses such as SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} and tightly coupled
INS innovation monitors~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor}
compare GNSS tracks with inertial dead reckoning.

\subsubsection*{Coordinated and distributed spoofing attacks}
Result~7 of~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} proves that a
formation-preserving group spoofing placement always exists (one
transmitter per impersonated satellite, placed along the satellite
direction); Jansen et al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} attempted a
distance-preserving four-USRP attack and failed beyond one receiver.
Ceccato et al.~\cite{ceccato2020spatial} designed a multi-antenna ground
spoofer with Wiener precoding that displaces a swarm without disrupting
its formation; Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025swarmspoofing} analyze
formation-preserving spoofing of small swarms;
GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp} demonstrated in hardware a
transmitter constellation, placed in line with the satellites at
altitude, that spoofs receivers over a wide area while preserving their
relative distances; and Park and Yoo~\cite{park2026rigidshift} state the
translation-invariance of relative-only detectors as a formal
proposition, rooted in the gauge freedom of network
localization~\cite{aspnes2006localization}. Surveys of the field
include~\cite{psiaki2016gnss,altaweel2023fanets,sebastian2026survey}.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Swarm, constellation, and measurement model}
We consider a swarm of $m$ UAVs flying in a square grid of
$\sqrt{m}\times\sqrt{m}$ elements with spacing $g$ (the \emph{formation
spacing}), following a way-point trajectory at altitudes between 100 and
700\,m that spans about 7\,km around its start point; positions are
sampled every $T_s=5$\,s. The GNSS constellation is modeled as
$N_{\mathrm{sat}}=24$ satellites on 6 circular orbital planes
(inclination $55^\circ$, altitude 20\,200\,km) over a rotating spherical
Earth. For UAV $u$ at position $\ptrue{u}(t)$ and satellite $s$ at
position $\mathbf{s}_s(t)$ (visible when its elevation is positive), the
authentic propagation delay is
$\tau_{u,s}(t)=\lVert\mathbf{s}_s(t)-\ptrue{u}(t)\rVert/c$. Each receiver
has a clock bias $\delta t_u\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)$\,s, constant over the
run, and each pseudorange carries additive white Gaussian noise of
standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{pr}}=3$\,m.

Each UAV computes its position fix $\pest{u}$ independently by iterated
linearized least squares (Gauss--Newton) over the four unknowns
$(x,y,z,\delta t)$: at iteration $k$, with predicted ranges
$R_s=\lVert\mathbf{s}_s-\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\rVert$ and pseudoranges
$\rho_s=c\,(\tilde{\tau}_{u,s}-\widehat{\delta t}^{(k)})$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{G}\,\Delta =\boldsymbol{\rho}-\mathbf{R},\qquad
\mathbf{G}_{s,:}=\Bigl[\tfrac{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{s}_s)^{\!\top}}{R_s},\; c\Bigr],
\label{eq:ls}
\end{equation}
solved in the least-squares sense for five iterations, where
$\tilde{\tau}_{u,s}$ is the \emph{measured} delay (authentic or spoofed).
We verified that, without attack and noise, the solver returns the true
grid positions to numerical precision (error $<10^{-4}$\,m), so
estimated and true positions live in the same frame.

In addition to GNSS, the UAVs measure their instantaneous mutual
distances with an independent on-board ranging sensor (e.g.\ UWB two-way
ranging~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}):
\begin{equation}
d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t) = \lVert\ptrue{i}(t)-\ptrue{j}(t)\rVert
 + \varepsilon_{ij}(t),\quad
\varepsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{rng}}),
\label{eq:uwb}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{\mathrm{rng}}=0.1$\,m. This is the most favorable setting
for the defender: the true formation is known essentially without error
at every epoch.

\subsection{Ground-to-UAV channel}
Spoofing signals travel over a ground-to-air link. We adopt the
suburban cellular-to-UAV path-loss model of Al-Hourani and
Gomez~\cite{alhourani2018cellular}: for a ground transmitter at
horizontal distance $d$ and elevation angle $\theta$ (in degrees) as seen
from the UAV,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PL}(d,\theta) = 10\,\alpha\log_{10}(d)
 + A\,(\theta-\theta_0)\,e^{-\frac{\theta-\theta_0}{B}} + \eta_0
 \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:alhourani}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=3.04$, $A=-23.29$, $B=4.14$, $\theta_0=-3.61^\circ$,
$\eta_0=20.70$\,dB (shadowing disabled for repeatability). The
excess-loss term is fitted in~\cite{alhourani2018cellular} for
$\theta\in[-2^\circ,10^\circ]$; larger angles are saturated at
$10^\circ$. With the array deployment of Section~\ref{sec:attack} no
link exceeded this limit in any run (saturation fraction $0.000$), so
the model is always used within its validity range. Authentic signals
follow free-space loss from satellite transmit power
$P_t^{\mathrm{sat}}=25$\,W with antenna gains
$G_t^{\mathrm{sat}}=13.5$\,dB and $G_r=5$\,dB, giving received
powers of about $-150$\,dBW.

\section{Physical Distributed Spoofing Attack}
\label{sec:attack}

The attacker's goal is to displace the whole swarm's perceived position
onto a decoy point (about 5.7~km from the flight path), while the swarm
believes it is following its mission plan. The attack is simulated
transmitter by transmitter; nothing is assumed about per-victim signal
shaping.

\subsection{Transmitter geometry and per-epoch routing}
$N_{\mathrm{sp}}=24$ fixed ground transmitters are placed on a circle of
radius $R_{\mathrm{sp}}=10$\,km enclosing the swarm's operating area
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}), each radiating $P_t^{\mathrm{sp}}=1$\,mW with
$G_t^{\mathrm{sp}}=1$\,dB. Following the geometric argument
of~\cite[Result~7]{tippenhauer2011requirements}---a formation-preserving
group spoofer uses one transmitter per impersonated satellite, as close
as possible to the satellite's direction---each visible satellite $s$ is
routed, at every epoch, to the transmitter whose azimuth as seen from the
\emph{current} true swarm centre $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}(t)$ is
closest to the satellite's azimuth:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sp}(s,t)=\arg\min_{k}\;
\bigl|\angle_{2\pi}\bigl(\phi_s(t)-\phi_k(t)\bigr)\bigr| .
\label{eq:assign}
\end{equation}
The transmitters never move; only the routing of satellites to
transmitters is updated, which requires the attacker to track the swarm
centre---the same knowledge it needs to calibrate the replayed delays.

\subsection{Capture and replayed delay}
For each UAV $u$ and visible satellite $s$ the receiver locks onto the
transmitter whenever the received spoofing power, computed
with~\eqref{eq:alhourani}, exceeds the authentic one. With the chosen
EIRP the spoofer margin over the satellite is between 13 and 29\,dB on
every link (median 19.5\,dB), so capture is complete and homogeneous
across the swarm (no UAV mixes authentic and spoofed satellites).

Transmitter $k=\mathrm{sp}(s,t)$ replays the signal of satellite $s$ with
its code phase calibrated so that, at the true swarm centre, the measured
delay equals the decoy delay $\tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}$ (the delay that
satellite $s$ would exhibit at the decoy position). A UAV at a different
position receives the same transmission over a different path, so it
measures
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\tau}_{u,s} \;=\; \tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}\;-\;\delta t_u\;+\;
\frac{\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\ptrue{u}\rVert-\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}\rVert}{c},
\label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{q}_k$ is the transmitter position and both norms are true
three-dimensional slant distances (altitude included). The last term is
the physical propagation residual: it is zero for the centre UAV, which
therefore computes exactly the decoy position, and nonzero for the other
UAVs.

\subsection{Residual non-rigidity of a ground array}
\label{sec:residual}
Write $\ptrue{u}=\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$ with
$\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}_u\rVert\ll\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}\rVert$.
To first order the residual in~\eqref{eq:power} is
$-(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_k\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)/c$, where
$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_k$ is the unit vector from the centre to the
transmitter, while the delay that would make UAV $u$ compute the decoy
position \emph{rigidly displaced} by $\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$ is
$-(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)/c$, with
$\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s$ the unit line of sight to the satellite. For a
horizontal swarm and azimuth-matched routing the per-satellite
pseudorange error is therefore
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{u,s}\;\approx\;
(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_s\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)\,
\bigl(\cos\theta_k-\cos\mathrm{el}_s\bigr),
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{a}}_s$ is the satellite azimuth direction, $\theta_k$
the (near-zero) elevation of the ground transmitter and $\mathrm{el}_s$
the satellite elevation. A ground transmitter cannot reproduce the
satellite's elevation, so~\eqref{eq:residual} vanishes only for
satellites near the horizon; it scales with the baseline and varies in
sign across satellites, and the least-squares solver turns it into a
position-dependent error, i.e.\ a \emph{deformation} of the computed
formation. Section~\ref{sec:results} measures this residual at about
22\,\% of the baseline. Two further facts, established numerically, are
relevant for an attacker: the residual does not depend on transmitter
power (capture is already complete at 1\,mW), and it depends critically
on the array enclosing the operating area---with a 2.5\,km array centred
on the start point the swarm is inside the circle only 26\,\% of the
time, and the deformation grows from 12\,\% inside to 55\,\% outside
(correlation $0.98$ with the distance from the array centre), because
from outside the array all transmitters lie in a narrow angular sector
and no routing can match the satellite azimuths.

\begin{remark}[Why collapse detectors are blind]
\label{rem:collapse}
The collapse detectors of Section~\ref{sec:detection} look for pairwise
distances that \emph{shrink} towards zero, the signature of a single
transmitter seen by all receivers~\cite[Result~1]{tippenhauer2011requirements}.
A distributed spoofer with one transmitter per satellite delivers to every
UAV a different set of delays, consistent to first order with its own
displacement from the centre: the computed formation is translated and
deformed by~\eqref{eq:residual}, but not collapsed. The statistic these
detectors threshold therefore never enters the alarm region, independently
of the threshold.
\end{remark}

\section{Relative-Distance Detection Methods}
\label{sec:detection}

All detectors operate on the normalized distance ratios
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}(t)=\frac{\hat{d}_{ij}(t)}{d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t)},
\qquad
\hat{d}_{ij}=\lVert\pest{i}-\pest{j}\rVert ,
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
computed over all $\binom{m}{2}=300$ pairs at every epoch, where
$d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}$ is the instantaneous UWB measurement
of~\eqref{eq:uwb}; under authentic conditions $r_{ij}\approx 1$.

\subsection{Collapse tests (Jansen et al.)}
The ACSAC~2016 detector~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} declares spoofing
when the pairwise distances \emph{shrink}:
\begin{equation}
f\bigl(\{r_{ij}\}\bigr)\;\underset{H_1}{\overset{H_0}{\gtrless}}\;\lambda,
\qquad \text{alarm if } f<\lambda,
\label{eq:jansen}
\end{equation}
with the three variants of its Appendix~A implemented here:
\begin{align}
f_{\min}  &= \min_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\max}  &= \max_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\mathrm{maj}} &= \operatorname*{med}_{i<j} r_{ij},
\label{eq:variants}
\end{align}
where the median form is equivalent to the paper's (normalized) majority
voting: at least half of the pairs below $\lambda$ iff the median is.
Jansen et al.\ set $\lambda$ from a Rician ($H_0$) / Rayleigh ($H_1$)
model of the pairwise distance with spatially correlated position noise,
$\sigma_d=\sqrt{2}\,\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}$; we calibrate $\lambda$
empirically to the same effect (Sec.~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsection{Formation consistency test}
As a representative of the two-sided GNSS-vs-ranging consistency family
--- $|d_{\mathrm{GPS}}-d_{\mathrm{UWB}}|>d_{\mathrm{thr}}$ for UAV
swarms~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}, double-difference GLRTs across
receivers~\cite{stenberg2022multiple}, GLRTs fusing GNSS fixes with
inter-vehicle ranges~\cite{swaszek2017platoon} --- we use the worst-pair
statistic
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{cons}} = \max_{i<j}\,\bigl|\,r_{ij}-1\,\bigr|,
\qquad \text{alarm if } T_{\mathrm{cons}}>\lambda .
\label{eq:cons}
\end{equation}
Unlike~\eqref{eq:jansen}, this test fires on \emph{any} deformation of
the formation, in either direction, and is therefore the natural
candidate against a distributed attacker.

\subsection{Threshold calibration and metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Following the Neyman--Pearson practice
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, thresholds are calibrated on the
$H_0$ (no-spoofing) scenario, simulated with the same noise levels:
$\lambda$ is the empirical $\PFA$-quantile (lower quantile for the
collapse tests, upper for the consistency test) of the statistic over all
$H_0$ epochs, with $\PFA=10^{-2}$. Performance is reported as (i) the
detection probability $\PD$, the fraction of attacked epochs that raise
an alarm; (ii) ROC curves obtained by sweeping $\lambda$; and (iii) the
median relative distance distortion
$E=\operatorname{med}_{i<j} |\hat{d}_{ij}-d_{ij}|/d_{ij}$, which
measures the geometric evidence available to any relative-distance
detector. Because the epochs of one mission share clocks, trajectory and
satellite geometry, the Monte Carlo unit is the \emph{mission}: 95\,\%
confidence intervals on $\PD$ are obtained by a paired bootstrap over
missions (500 resamples; $H_0$ and attack of a mission share the random
seed, and the threshold is re-calibrated in every resample).

\subsection{Formation distortion}
Figure~\ref{fig:distortion} reports the median relative distance
distortion $E$ over the 20 missions (band: 10th--90th percentile across

First, \textbf{the collapse detectors are defeated at every spacing}:
all three Jansen variants give $\PD\le0.011$ (95\,\% upper bound), i.e.\
at or below the false-alarm rate, from 5 to 100\,m, and their ROC curves
lie on or below the chance diagonal. This is the consequence of
Remark~\ref{rem:collapse}: the distributed attack translates and deforms
the formation but never collapses it, so the one-sided statistic
of~\eqref{eq:jansen} never crosses its threshold; the majority variant,
the one recommended in~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, never fires at
all. No threshold choice changes this, since the attacked statistic is
displaced \emph{away} from the alarm region.

Second, \textbf{the consistency test retains partial power that grows
with the formation spacing}: $\PD$ rises from $0.03$ at 5\,m and $0.11$
at 10\,m to $0.56$ at 50\,m and $0.65$ at 100\,m, with wide confidence
intervals that reflect the mission-to-mission variability of the
residual. The test sees the 22\,\% deformation only where it exceeds the
$1/g$ noise floor; for compact swarms, the regime of interest for
formation flight, the attack passes essentially undetected at
$\PFA=10^{-2}$, and even at 100\,m one attacked epoch in three raises no
alarm.

Third, \textbf{the residual is the only handle the defender has}. It is
not a tuning artifact of the attack (power does not change it) and it is
not removable by routing once the array encloses the operating area; it
is the geometric cost of keeping the transmitters on the ground, and its
magnitude is governed by the satellite elevations through
\eqref{eq:residual}.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsubsection*{Scope of the result}
The evaluation covers detectors that are functions of relative
position/distance quantities---the collapse family and the two-sided
consistency family. The former is blind to the attack for a structural
reason (Remark~\ref{rem:collapse}); the latter is limited only by the
ratio between the ground-array residual and the GNSS noise, and would
detect the attack reliably for wide formations, low-noise (e.g.\ RTK)
fixes, or longer integration windows. The result does \emph{not} extend
to (i) \emph{direction-of-arrival} detectors: a circle of ground
transmitters produces arrivals at near-zero elevation, incongruent with
the satellite constellation, so multi-antenna arrays with attitude
information~\cite{psiaki2016gnss,borio2016sumofsquares} would see our
attacker, unless its transmitters are placed along the satellite
directions as in~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}; nor to (ii)
\emph{absolute-reference} checks---ranging to fixed infrastructure, TDoA
from ground sensor networks~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec}, terrain or
signal-of-opportunity positioning---for which the 4\,km displacement is
directly observable; nor to (iii) GNSS/INS detectors such as
SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} and INS innovation
monitors~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor}, which respond to
the time variation of the induced error and were not evaluated here.

\subsubsection*{Relation to formation-preserving attacks}
Tippenhauer et al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} prove that a
formation-preserving placement exists and GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}
realizes it with transmitters in line with the satellites at altitude;
Park and Yoo~\cite{park2026rigidshift} show that an exactly common
translation is invisible to any relative-only detector. Our attacker is
deliberately more constrained---fixed transmitters on the ground---and
the quantity we measure is precisely what separates it from those
idealized attacks: a residual deformation of about 22\,\% of the
baseline, enough to be seen by a two-sided test on wide formations and
too small to be seen on compact ones. Raising the transmitters would
reduce the residual according to~\eqref{eq:residual}; we leave that
trade-off to future work.

\subsubsection*{Deployment and steady state}
The result assumes an array that encloses the swarm's operating area
(Section~\ref{sec:residual}); a static array that the swarm leaves
deforms the formation by more than 50\,\% and is readily detected. The
decoy position is constant, so the attack is modeled in its steady state
after capture; the transient at capture and time-varying decoys are not
modeled.

\subsubsection*{Other simplifications}
The simulator uses a spherical Earth, a synthetic constellation,
uncorrelated pseudorange noise (spatial correlation would lower the
detector's noise floor~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} and favour the
consistency test), no multipath or shadowing, and signal capture by power
comparison without tracking-loop dynamics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We evaluated the classical multi-receiver relative-distance spoofing
detectors against a physical distributed ground spoofer attacking a UAV
swarm, simulated transmitter by transmitter without any idealization of
the attack. The attack displaces the swarm by 4\,km and defeats the
collapse detectors of Jansen et al.\ in all their variants at every
formation spacing ($\PD\le0.011$), because a distributed spoofer does not
produce the collapse signature these detectors rely on. A two-sided
consistency check against independent ranging retains partial power
only for wide formations ($\PD=0.65$ at 100\,m, $0.03$ at 5\,m), limited
by a residual deformation of about 22\,\% of the baseline that is the
geometric cost of ground-based transmitters. Robust swarm navigation
therefore cannot rely on collapse detection, and relative consistency
checks should be complemented by direction-of-arrival or
absolute-reference information.
