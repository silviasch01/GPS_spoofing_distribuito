\begin{abstract}
Multi-receiver spoofing detection is widely regarded as one of the most
robust countermeasures against civilian GNSS spoofing: receivers deployed
in a known formation compare the mutual distances implied by their GNSS
fixes with the known (or independently measured) formation geometry, and a
single-transmitter spoofer, which collapses all victims onto the same
position, is detected with near-certain probability. In this paper we
evaluate this detector family against a \emph{distributed} spoofer:
multiple ground transmitters deployed around the target area, each
impersonating the satellite closest to it in azimuth. We develop a
physical simulation model of a 25-UAV swarm overflown by 24 ground
spoofers, including an elevation-dependent ground-to-UAV channel, and we
implement the collapse test of Jansen et al.\ (ACSAC~2016) in its three
variants together with a two-sided formation-consistency test
representative of GNSS-vs-ranging checks. Our results quantify a
\emph{coordination gap}: the uncoordinated distributed attack distorts the
swarm formation by 42--56\,\% of the baseline and is detected with
probability up to $0.98$ at a $10^{-2}$ false-alarm rate, whereas its
coordinated counterpart---which imposes a common rigid translation on
every receiver---preserves all pairwise distances exactly and reduces
every detector in the family to chance level
($\PD \approx \PFA$ at every formation spacing from 5 to 100\,m, ROC on
the diagonal). The blindness is structural: any statistic computed from
relative quantities only is invariant under a common translation. We
position this result with respect to recent formal and experimental
statements of the same limit, delimit its scope (direction-of-arrival and
absolute-reference detectors are \emph{not} blind to our ground
configuration), and release the full MATLAB simulator.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% TODO (autore): riscrivere/estendere l'introduzione. Questa e' una bozza
%% minima per rendere il documento compilabile e coerente.

Civilian GNSS signals are unauthenticated and can be forged by
commodity software-defined radios~\cite{humphreys2008assessing}. Among
the proposed countermeasures, \emph{multi-receiver} (or multi-agent)
detection stands out for its simplicity: since a single-antenna spoofer
forces every victim in its coverage area to compute the same position
fix~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements}, a group of receivers that knows
its own formation can detect the attack by checking that the mutual
distances implied by the GNSS fixes remain consistent with the formation
geometry~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver,swaszek2013multicots}. The same
principle underpins recent proposals for UAV swarms, which compare
GNSS-derived inter-drone distances against independent ultra-wideband
(UWB) ranging~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb,bi2024sdp}.

This paper asks whether the guarantee survives a \emph{distributed}
attacker, i.e.\ several coordinated ground transmitters surrounding the
target area. Our contributions are:
(i) a physical simulation model of a distributed ground spoofer attacking
a moving UAV swarm, including per-satellite spoofer assignment and an
elevation-dependent ground-to-UAV channel~\cite{alhourani2018cellular};
(ii) a faithful implementation of the collapse detector of Jansen et
al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} and of a two-sided formation
consistency test~\cite{stenberg2022multiple,mykytyn2023uwb};
(iii) a quantitative evaluation showing that the \emph{uncoordinated}
distributed attack is detectable, while its \emph{coordinated}
(rigid-translation) counterpart drives every detector of the family to
chance level; and
(iv) an honest delimitation of the result with respect to
direction-of-arrival and absolute-reference defenses.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% TODO (autore): riscrivere. Bozza strutturata con le citazioni chiave.

\subsubsection*{Multi-receiver position-domain detection}
Tippenhauer et al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} formalized the GPS
group-spoofing problem and proposed checking that the victims' computed
positions preserve their physical formation; Jansen et
al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} turned the check into a statistical
test on pairwise distances with Rician/Rayleigh error models and
spatially correlated noise, and Swaszek and
Hartnett~\cite{swaszek2013multicots} derived Neyman--Pearson optimal
variants for COTS receivers. Crowd-GPS-Sec~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec}
applies the same collapse principle to crowdsourced air-traffic
receivers.

\subsubsection*{Measurement-domain and swarm variants}
Stenberg et al.~\cite{stenberg2022multiple} test double differences of
pseudorange and carrier phase across receivers ($\chi^2$/GLRT), Kalantari
and Larsson~\cite{kalantari2020rigidbody} derive a GLRT for receivers on
a rigid body with unknown pose explicitly against multiple spoofers, and
Borio and Gioia~\cite{borio2016sumofsquares} detect the single common
direction of arrival with a two-receiver sum-of-squares statistic. For
UAV swarms, Mykytyn et al.~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} compare GNSS distances
with UWB two-way ranging, and Bi et al.~\cite{bi2024sdp} cast detection
as a semidefinite localization-feasibility problem. GNSS/INS fusion
defenses such as SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} and tightly coupled
INS innovation monitors~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor}
compare GNSS tracks with inertial dead reckoning.

\subsubsection*{Coordinated spoofing attacks}
Result~7 of~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} proves that a
formation-preserving group spoofing placement always exists (one
transmitter per impersonated satellite); Jansen et
al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} attempted a distance-preserving
four-USRP attack and failed beyond one receiver. Ceccato et
al.~\cite{ceccato2020spatial} designed a multi-antenna ground spoofer
with Wiener precoding that displaces a swarm without disrupting its
formation; Chen et al.~\cite{chen2024swarmspoofing} analyze
formation-preserving spoofing of small swarms;
GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp} demonstrated in hardware a
transmitter constellation that spoofs receivers over a wide area while
preserving their relative distances, explicitly bypassing
multi-receiver~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} and
sensor-fusion~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} countermeasures; and Park and
Yoo~\cite{park2026rigidshift} state the translation-invariance of
relative-only detectors as a formal proposition, rooted in the gauge
freedom of network localization~\cite{aspnes2006localization}. Surveys
of the field include~\cite{psiaki2016gnss,altaweel2023fanets,%
sebastian2026survey}.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Swarm, constellation, and measurement model}
We consider a swarm of $m$ UAVs flying in a rigid square grid of
$\sqrt{m}\times\sqrt{m}$ elements with spacing $g$ (the \emph{formation
spacing}), following a way-point trajectory at altitudes between 100 and
700\,m; positions are sampled every $T_s=5$\,s. The GNSS constellation is
modeled as $N_{\mathrm{sat}}=24$ satellites on 6 circular orbital planes
(inclination $55^\circ$, altitude 20\,200\,km) over a rotating spherical
Earth. For UAV $u$ at position $\ptrue{u}(t)$ and satellite $s$ at
position $\mathbf{s}_s(t)$ (visible when its elevation is positive), the
nominal propagation delay is
$\tau_{u,s}(t)=\lVert\mathbf{s}_s(t)-\ptrue{u}(t)\rVert/c$. Each receiver
has a clock bias $\delta t_u\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)$\,s, constant over the
run, and each pseudorange carries additive white Gaussian noise of
standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{pr}}=3$\,m.

Each UAV computes its position fix $\pest{u}$ independently by iterated
linearized least squares (Gauss--Newton) over the four unknowns
$(x,y,z,\delta t)$: at iteration $k$, with predicted ranges
$R_s=\lVert\mathbf{s}_s-\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\rVert$ and pseudoranges
$\rho_s=c\,(\tilde{\tau}_{u,s}-\widehat{\delta t}^{(k)})$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{G}\,\Delta =\boldsymbol{\rho}-\mathbf{R},\qquad
\mathbf{G}_{s,:}=\Bigl[\tfrac{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{s}_s)^{\!\top}}{R_s},\; c\Bigr],
\label{eq:ls}
\end{equation}
solved in the least-squares sense for five iterations, where
$\tilde{\tau}_{u,s}$ is the \emph{measured} delay (authentic or spoofed).

In addition to GNSS, the UAVs measure their true mutual distances with an
independent on-board ranging sensor (e.g.\ UWB two-way
ranging~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}):
\begin{equation}
d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t) = \lVert\ptrue{i}(t)-\ptrue{j}(t)\rVert
 + \varepsilon_{ij}(t),\quad
\varepsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{rng}}),
\label{eq:uwb}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{\mathrm{rng}}=0.1$\,m. This is the most favorable setting
for the defender: the formation geometry is known essentially without
error.

\subsection{Ground-to-UAV channel}
Spoofing signals travel over a ground-to-air link. We adopt the
suburban cellular-to-UAV path-loss model of Al-Hourani and
Gomez~\cite{alhourani2018cellular}: for a ground transmitter at distance
$d$ and depression/elevation angle $\theta$ (in degrees) as seen from the
UAV,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PL}(d,\theta) = 10\,\alpha\log_{10}(d)
 + A\,(\theta-\theta_0)\,e^{-\frac{\theta-\theta_0}{B}} + \eta_0
 \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:alhourani}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=3.04$, $A=-23.29$, $B=4.14$, $\theta_0=-3.61^\circ$,
$\eta_0=20.70$\,dB (shadowing disabled for repeatability). Authentic
signals follow free-space loss from satellite transmit power
$P_t^{\mathrm{sat}}=25$\,W with antenna gains
$G_t^{\mathrm{sat}}=13.5$\,dB and $G_r=5$\,dB.

\section{Distributed Spoofing Attack}
\label{sec:attack}

The attacker's goal is to displace the whole swarm's perceived position
onto a target trajectory (a fixed decoy point in our runs, roughly 5.7~km
from the flight path), while the swarm believes it is following its
mission plan.

\subsection{Transmitter geometry and satellite assignment}
$N_{\mathrm{sp}}=24$ ground transmitters are placed on a circle of radius
$R_{\mathrm{sp}}=2.5$\,km centered on the target area
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}), each radiating $P_t^{\mathrm{sp}}=1$\,mW with
$G_t^{\mathrm{sp}}=1$\,dB. Following the geometric feasibility argument
of~\cite[Result~7]{tippenhauer2011requirements}---a formation-preserving
group spoofing placement uses one transmitter per impersonated
satellite---each visible satellite $s$ is assigned to the spoofer whose
azimuth, as seen from the area center, is closest to the satellite's
azimuth:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sp}(s,t)=\arg\min_{k}\;
\bigl|\angle_{2\pi}\bigl(\phi_s(t)-\phi_k\bigr)\bigr|,
\label{eq:assign}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_s$ is the satellite azimuth and $\phi_k$ the azimuth of
spoofer $k$.

\subsection{Uncoordinated attack (\textsf{power})}
In the baseline attack the spoofers transmit a common signal set, with no
per-victim adaptation. For each UAV $u$ and satellite $s$, the receiver
locks onto the spoofer whenever the received spoofing power exceeds the
authentic one, $P_r^{\mathrm{sp}}>P_r^{\mathrm{sat}}$, with
$P_r^{\mathrm{sp}}$ given by~\eqref{eq:alhourani}. The measured delay is
then
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\tau}_{u,s} \;=\; \tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}
 \;-\;\delta t_u\;-\;\frac{R_{\mathrm{sp}}-d_{u,\mathrm{sp}(s)}}{c},
\label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
where $\tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}$ is the delay that satellite $s$ would
exhibit at the decoy position and $d_{u,\mathrm{sp}(s)}$ is the true
spoofer-to-UAV distance. The last term is the physical propagation from
the assigned spoofer: it varies across the swarm along the
\emph{horizontal} direction towards the spoofer, whereas an authentic
delay varies along the inclined line of sight to the satellite. This
mismatch makes the induced position offset \emph{position-dependent}, so
the swarm is displaced but also \emph{deformed}.

\subsection{Coordinated attack (\textsf{rigid})}
The coordinated attacker knows the swarm geometry (or equivalently,
adapts per victim) and pre-compensates the per-UAV terms so that every
receiver measures delays consistent with its \emph{true} position shifted
by a common vector:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\tau}_{u,s} \;=\;
\frac{\lVert \mathbf{s}_s-(\ptrue{u}+\boldsymbol{\Delta})\rVert}{c}
\;-\;\delta t_u ,
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Delta}(t)=\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{dec}}(t)-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}(t),
\label{eq:rigid}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}$ and $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ are the
true and decoy trajectories of the swarm center. Solving~\eqref{eq:ls}
then yields $\pest{u}=\ptrue{u}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}+\mathbf{n}_u$: the
whole swarm is rigidly translated. The transmitters are \emph{not}
moved; \eqref{eq:rigid} models the delays that the coordinated ground
array must synthesize, i.e.\ the ideal end state of the attack
of~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements,tibaldo2025gnsswasp}. The gap
between \eqref{eq:power} and \eqref{eq:rigid} is precisely the
coordination effort required of the attacker, and Section~\ref{sec:results}
quantifies its observable consequence.

\subsection{Why rigid translations are undetectable}
\label{sec:nullspace}

\begin{proposition}[Translation invariance]
\label{prop:null}
Let $\pest{u}=\ptrue{u}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}+\mathbf{n}_u$,
$u=1,\dots,m$, with $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ common to all receivers, and
let the detector statistic be any measurable function
\begin{equation}
T=f\bigl(\{\pest{i}-\pest{j}\}_{i<j},\,
         \{d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}\}_{i<j}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Then $T$ has the same distribution under $H_0$
($\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\mathbf{0}$) and under the rigid attack
($\boldsymbol{\Delta}\neq\mathbf{0}$); consequently
$\PD=\PFA$ for every threshold.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
$\pest{i}-\pest{j}=(\ptrue{i}-\ptrue{j})+(\mathbf{n}_i-\mathbf{n}_j)$
does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$, and
$d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}$ is measured independently of GNSS. Hence the
joint distribution of the arguments of $f$, and therefore of $T$, is
identical under the two hypotheses.
\end{IEEEproof}

If the statistic uses only distances (not vectors), invariance extends to
the whole Euclidean group (common rotations and reflections). The
proposition restates, in the spoofing context, the gauge freedom of
anchor-free network localization~\cite{aspnes2006localization}; it
appears implicitly in~\cite[Result~7]{tippenhauer2011requirements} and
explicitly in~\cite{park2026rigidshift}, and is realized in hardware
by~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}. Our contribution is not the statement but
its quantitative confrontation with a \emph{physical ground-based}
attacker model.

\section{Relative-Distance Detection Methods}
\label{sec:detection}

All detectors operate on the normalized distance ratios
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}(t)=\frac{\hat{d}_{ij}(t)}{d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t)},
\qquad
\hat{d}_{ij}=\lVert\pest{i}-\pest{j}\rVert ,
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
computed over all $\binom{m}{2}=300$ pairs at every epoch; under
authentic conditions $r_{ij}\approx 1$.

\subsection{Collapse tests (Jansen et al.)}
The ACSAC~2016 detector~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} declares spoofing
when the pairwise distances \emph{shrink}---the signature of a
single-transmitter spoofer that collapses all fixes onto one point:
\begin{equation}
f\bigl(\{r_{ij}\}\bigr)\;\underset{H_1}{\overset{H_0}{\gtrless}}\;\lambda,
\qquad \text{alarm if } f<\lambda,
\label{eq:jansen}
\end{equation}
with the three variants of its Appendix~A implemented here:
\begin{align}
f_{\min}  &= \min_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\max}  &= \max_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\mathrm{maj}} &= \operatorname*{med}_{i<j} r_{ij},
\label{eq:variants}
\end{align}
where the median form is equivalent to the paper's (normalized) majority
voting: at least half of the pairs below $\lambda$ iff the median is.
Jansen et al.\ set $\lambda$ from a Rician ($H_0$) / Rayleigh ($H_1$)
model of the pairwise distance with spatially correlated position noise,
$\sigma_d=\sqrt{2}\,\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}$; we calibrate $\lambda$
empirically to the same effect (Sec.~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsection{Formation consistency test}
As a representative of the two-sided GNSS-vs-ranging consistency family
--- $|d_{\mathrm{GPS}}-d_{\mathrm{UWB}}|>d_{\mathrm{thr}}$ for UAV
swarms~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}, double-difference GLRTs across
receivers~\cite{stenberg2022multiple}, GLRTs fusing GNSS fixes with
inter-vehicle ranges~\cite{swaszek2017platoon} --- we use the worst-pair
statistic
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{cons}} = \max_{i<j}\,\bigl|\,r_{ij}-1\,\bigr|,
\qquad \text{alarm if } T_{\mathrm{cons}}>\lambda .
\label{eq:cons}
\end{equation}
Unlike~\eqref{eq:jansen}, this test fires on \emph{any} deformation of
the formation, in either direction, and is therefore the natural
candidate against a distributed attacker that scatters rather than
collapses the fixes.

\subsection{Threshold calibration and metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Following the Neyman--Pearson practice
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, thresholds are calibrated on the
$H_0$ (no-spoofing) scenario, simulated with the same noise levels:
$\lambda$ is the empirical $\PFA$-quantile (lower quantile for the
collapse tests, upper for the consistency test) of the statistic over all
$H_0$ epochs, with $\PFA=10^{-2}$. Performance is then reported as (i)
the detection probability $\PD$, the fraction of attacked epochs that
raise an alarm; (ii) full ROC curves obtained by sweeping $\lambda$; and
(iii) the median relative distance distortion
$E=\operatorname{med}_{i<j} |\hat{d}_{ij}-d_{ij}|/d_{ij}$, which
measures the geometric evidence available to any relative-distance
detector.

\subsection{Attack effectiveness}
Both attacks succeed at displacing the swarm: under \textsf{rigid} the
center UAV's estimated position is dragged by the full decoy offset
(4.0\,km on average over the mission) at every epoch, and under
\textsf{power} a comparable displacement (4.6\,km on average) is
achieved (Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}, top). The difference is in what the attacks
do to the swarm's \emph{shape} (Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}, bottom): the
uncoordinated attack deforms the formation persistently, while the
coordinated attack leaves the relative geometry statistically
indistinguishable from the no-attack case.

\subsection{Formation distortion}
Figure~\ref{fig:distortion} reports the median relative distance
distortion $E$. Under \textsf{H0} the distortion is pure GNSS noise and
decays as $1/g$ (from 18\,\% at $g=5$\,m to 0.8\,\% at 100\,m, with
$\sigma_{\mathrm{pr}}=3$\,m). The \textsf{rigid} attack is
\emph{identical} to \textsf{H0} to the fourth significant digit at every
spacing---the common translation cancels exactly in every pairwise
difference, as predicted by Proposition~\ref{prop:null}. The
\textsf{power} attack instead distorts the formation by 42--56\,\%
regardless of spacing: the per-UAV propagation term
in~\eqref{eq:power} scales with the baseline, so the deformation never
averages out and, for $g\gtrsim20$\,m, towers over the noise floor.

First, the \textbf{uncoordinated attack is detectable}. The consistency
test~\eqref{eq:cons} reaches $\PD=0.73$ at $g=50$\,m and $0.98$ at
100\,m, where the 50\,\% deformation dwarfs the noise; below 20\,m the
noise floor masks it. The Jansen variants detect \textsf{power} only
partially ($\PD\approx0.65$--$0.80$): they are one-sided collapse tests,
and the distributed attack \emph{scatters} the fixes instead of
collapsing them---indeed $f_{\max}$, which requires every pair to
shrink, barely fires at all ($\PD\le0.66$).

Second, the \textbf{coordinated attack is invisible}: for all four
detectors and all spacings, $\PD\in\{0.008,0.010\}\approx\PFA$, and the
ROC curves for \textsf{rigid} lie on the chance diagonal
(Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}). This is not a tuning artifact: by
Proposition~\ref{prop:null} no threshold, window length, or variant of a
relative-only statistic can do better.

Third, the comparison quantifies the \textbf{coordination gap}: the
42--56\,\% residual deformation of~\eqref{eq:power} is what currently
separates a physical ground array from the null space. An attacker able
to apply the per-victim pre-compensation of~\eqref{eq:rigid}---which
requires knowledge of each UAV's position to within the detector's noise
floor, i.e.\ metres---closes the gap entirely.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsubsection*{Scope of the blindness claim}
Proposition~\ref{prop:null} covers detectors that are functions of
relative position/distance quantities---the collapse and consistency
families evaluated here, the double-difference and rigid-body GLRTs
of~\cite{stenberg2022multiple,kalantari2020rigidbody}, the swarm
GNSS-vs-UWB checks of~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb,bi2024sdp}---and, in the time
domain, GNSS/INS detectors built on displacement consistency: the
maneuver statistic of SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} is computed on
displacements from the maneuver start, so a constant
$\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ cancels identically, and in tightly coupled INS
monitors a constant offset absorbed at filter initialization produces
zero innovation~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor}. It does
\emph{not} cover (i) \emph{direction-of-arrival} detectors: a circle of
ground transmitters produces arrivals at near-zero elevation,
incongruent with the satellite constellation, so multi-antenna arrays
with attitude information would detect our attacker unless its
transmitters are placed along the satellite directions as
in~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}; nor (ii) \emph{absolute-reference}
checks---ranging to fixed infrastructure, TDoA from ground sensor
networks~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec}, terrain or signal-of-opportunity
positioning---for which the common translation is directly observable.
A complete defense should therefore anchor at least one absolute
reference, consistent with the anchor requirements derived
in~\cite{park2026rigidshift}.

\subsubsection*{Idealized rigid model}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rigid} prescribes the delays of a \emph{perfectly}
coordinated array; it is an upper bound for the attacker, adopted
deliberately so that the detector-side conclusion is threshold-free. A
physical ground array approximates it with a residual non-rigidity that
grows with swarm aperture and shrinks with transmitter altitude and
range---GNSS-WASP measured sub-metre residuals at 1\,km in
hardware~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}, below every published formation
threshold (e.g.\ ${\approx}5$\,m~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb},
${\approx}3.6$\,m~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}). Mapping the residual
of \emph{our} ground-circle geometry as a function of radius, altitude,
and swarm extent is the natural next step; the \textsf{power} results
already bound it from above.

\subsubsection*{Other simplifications}
The simulator uses a spherical Earth, a synthetic constellation,
uncorrelated pseudorange noise (spatial correlation would \emph{lower}
the detector's noise floor~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, strengthening
the \textsf{power} detection result but leaving
Proposition~\ref{prop:null} untouched), no multipath, and signal capture
by simple power comparison without tracking-loop dynamics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We evaluated the classical multi-receiver relative-distance spoofing
detectors against a distributed ground spoofer attacking a UAV swarm.
An uncoordinated 24-transmitter attack displaces the swarm but deforms
its formation by ${\sim}50\%$ and is detected (up to $\PD=0.98$ at
$\PFA=10^{-2}$); the same attack with per-victim coordination imposes a
common rigid translation, preserves every pairwise distance, and reduces
the entire detector family---Jansen's collapse variants and the
formation-consistency test---to chance level at every formation spacing,
as dictated by a simple invariance argument. The practical security of
relative-distance detection thus rests not on its statistic but on the
attacker's coordination cost, which recent hardware results are rapidly
eroding; robust swarm navigation requires anchoring to at least one
absolute position reference.
