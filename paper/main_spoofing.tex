\begin{abstract}
Multi-receiver spoofing detection rests on a geometric fact: a
single-transmitter spoofer forces every receiver of a formation onto the
same position, so the mutual distances implied by the GNSS fixes
collapse. We ask what a \emph{physical distributed} spoofer does
instead---24 fixed ground transmitters around the operating area of a
25-UAV swarm, each replaying the satellite closest to it in azimuth,
with capture decided by received power through an elevation-dependent
ground-to-UAV channel and the replayed code phase propagating over the
true three-dimensional path to every drone---and what the
relative-distance detectors see. Simulating the attack transmitter by
transmitter over the 5--20\,m inter-UAV spacing of outdoor GPS-based
formation flight, with 20 Monte Carlo missions per configuration, we
find that a ground array cannot preserve the formation: because its
transmitters cannot reproduce the satellites' elevation, the computed
formation is \emph{dilated} by about 23\,\% of the baseline, a value
predicted to within a few percent, epoch by epoch, by a first-order
least-squares model of the residual. This dilation is invisible to the
collapse detectors of Jansen et al.\ (ACSAC~2016), which test the wrong
tail ($\PD\le0.011$ at $\PFA=10^{-2}$, ROC area down to $0.001$), and
is largely masked by GNSS noise for the worst-pair GNSS-vs-UWB check
used in swarm detectors ($\PD=0.03$--$0.26$); but a two-sided test on
the median distance ratio detects it at every spacing
($\PD=0.38$ at 5\,m to $0.98$ at 20\,m, AUC $0.83$--$0.999$). The
result replaces the collapse signature with a dilation signature as the
quantity relative-distance detectors should test for against
distributed ground spoofers, and delimits its scope with respect to
direction-of-arrival and absolute-reference defenses.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
GNSS spoofing, UAV swarms, spoofing detection, multi-receiver, distributed
attack, relative distances, formation flight.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Civilian GNSS signals carry no authentication: their structure and
navigation message are public, and a transmitter that reproduces them
with enough power can make a receiver compute any position and time of
the attacker's choosing. Since the first portable civilian spoofer was
demonstrated~\cite{humphreys2008assessing}, software-defined radios and
open-source signal generators have made the attack accessible for a few
hundred dollars, and incidents affecting ships, aircraft and unmanned
systems are reported routinely~\cite{psiaki2016gnss}. Unmanned aerial
vehicles (UAVs) are a natural target: they navigate on GNSS by default,
and a swarm flying in formation---for surveying, inspection, delivery,
or defense---multiplies both the value of the target and the
consequences of a successful hijack.

Among the countermeasures proposed in the last fifteen
years~\cite{psiaki2016gnss,altaweel2023fanets}, \emph{multi-receiver}
detection stands out for its simplicity and for the strength of its
guarantee. Its principle, established by Tippenhauer et
al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements}, is geometric: a spoofer with a
single antenna delivers the same signals to every receiver in its
coverage area, so all the victims compute the \emph{same} position fix.
A group of receivers that knows its own formation can therefore detect
the attack by checking that the mutual distances implied by the GNSS
fixes have not collapsed. Jansen et al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}
turned the check into a calibrated statistical test, showing that four
receivers a few metres apart reach a detection probability of $0.99$ at
a false-alarm rate of $10^{-5}$; Swaszek and
Hartnett~\cite{swaszek2013multicots} derived its Neyman--Pearson optimal
form. For UAV swarms the same idea has been proposed with the formation
measured on-line by inter-drone ultra-wideband (UWB)
ranging~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} or verified as a localization feasibility
problem~\cite{bi2024sdp}. The guarantee rests on one assumption: that
the attacker has a single transmission point.

This paper asks what happens when the assumption is dropped in the most
concrete way possible---not with an idealized attacker that imposes a
common offset on every victim, but with a \emph{physical distributed}
spoofer: several fixed ground transmitters surrounding the swarm's
operating area, each impersonating one satellite, subject to real
propagation geometry and a realistic ground-to-UAV channel. Such an
attacker is within reach of a well-resourced adversary, and
formation-preserving spoofing has been demonstrated in hardware when the
transmitters are placed along the satellite directions at
altitude~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}. Ground-based transmitters, however,
cannot reproduce the satellites' elevation; what they do to the computed
formation, and which relative-distance test sees it, has not been
quantified.

We answer by simulation over the inter-UAV spacing of 5 to 20\,m that
characterizes outdoor GPS-based formation flight
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a transmitter-by-transmitter simulation model of a distributed
  ground spoofer attacking a moving UAV swarm, with per-epoch routing of
  satellites to transmitters, true three-dimensional propagation of the
  replayed signals, and the elevation-dependent ground-to-UAV path-loss
  model of~\cite{alhourani2018cellular} (Section~\ref{sec:attack});
\item the finding, with a validated first-order model, that a ground
  array \emph{dilates} the computed formation by about
  $1/\langle\cos\mathrm{el}\rangle-1\approx23\,\%$ of the baseline,
  because of the mismatch between satellite and transmitter elevations
  (Section~\ref{sec:residual});
\item a Monte Carlo evaluation with mission-level bootstrap confidence
  intervals of five relative-distance detectors---the three collapse
  variants of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, the worst-pair
  GNSS-vs-ranging check~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb,stenberg2022multiple}, and
  a two-sided test on the median distance ratio---showing that the
  dilation escapes the first four and is detected by the fifth at every
  spacing (Section~\ref{sec:results});
\item a delimitation of the result with respect to direction-of-arrival
  and absolute-reference defenses (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
The simulator and the detection code are released with the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Multi-receiver detection in the position domain}
Tippenhauer et al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} formalized the
GPS group-spoofing problem and proved two complementary results: a
single-antenna spoofer places all victims at the same location
(Result~1), and a formation-preserving placement of one transmitter per
impersonated satellite always exists, along the satellite directions
(Result~7). Their countermeasure checks that the computed positions
preserve the physical formation, without a statistical model. Jansen et
al.~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} supplied the model: the pairwise
distances between receivers are Rician under authentic conditions and
Rayleigh under a single-transmitter attack, with spatially correlated
position noise ($\sigma\approx0.5$--$3$\,m, $\rho\approx0.4$--$0.6$
measured), and the test $f(\{d_{ij}\})<\lambda$ is evaluated in minimal,
maximal, majority and normalized-majority variants, the last being the
best. Their prototype with four receivers on a 5\,m square ran for three
hours without errors; their own attempt at a distance-preserving
four-USRP attack succeeded on one receiver and failed on two. Swaszek
and Hartnett~\cite{swaszek2013multicots} derived Neyman--Pearson optimal
tests for COTS receivers at known relative positions, and later fused
GNSS fixes with inter-vehicle ranges for platoons under the assumption
that only one vehicle is spoofed~\cite{swaszek2017platoon}.
Crowd-GPS-Sec~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec} applies the collapse
principle to crowdsourced air-traffic receivers, combined with an
absolute time-difference-of-arrival reference.

\subsection{Measurement-domain and swarm variants}
In the measurement domain, Stenberg et al.~\cite{stenberg2022multiple}
test double differences of pseudorange and carrier phase between
receivers with a $\chi^2$ GLRT, finding a minimum receiver separation of
about 5\,m with standard positioning; Kalantari and
Larsson~\cite{kalantari2020rigidbody} derive a GLRT for receivers on a
rigid body with unknown pose explicitly against multiple spoofers,
showing that more receivers make the attack harder; Borio and
Gioia~\cite{borio2016sumofsquares} detect the single common direction of
arrival with a two-receiver carrier-phase sum-of-squares statistic. For
UAV swarms, Mykytyn et al.~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} compare GNSS-derived
inter-drone distances with UWB two-way ranging and raise an alarm when
they differ by more than about 5\,m, dismissing a coordinated per-drone
attack as "virtually impossible"; Bi et al.~\cite{bi2024sdp} cast
detection as a semidefinite localization-feasibility problem robust to
colluding members. GNSS/INS fusion defenses such as
SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} and tightly coupled INS innovation
monitors~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor} compare the GNSS
track with inertial dead reckoning and respond to the time variation of
the induced error.

\subsection{Coordinated and distributed spoofing}
Attack-side work has progressively approached the formation-preserving
placement of~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements}. Ceccato et
al.~\cite{ceccato2020spatial} designed a multi-antenna ground spoofer
with Wiener precoding that displaces a swarm without disrupting its
formation, at the level of a mathematical model; Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2025swarmspoofing} analyze formation-preserving spoofing of
small swarms with software-defined radios;
GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp} demonstrated in hardware a
constellation of at least four GPSDO-synchronized transmitters, placed
in line with the satellites at altitude, that spoofs an arbitrary number
of receivers over a square kilometre while preserving their relative
distances to within a metre, explicitly bypassing
multi-receiver~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} and
sensor-fusion~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} countermeasures; its authors
note that the residual bias of a real transmitter constellation
"cannot be eliminated unless they are placed in orbit". Park and
Yoo~\cite{park2026rigidshift} state the translation-invariance of
relative-only detectors as a formal proposition, rooted in the gauge
freedom of anchor-free network
localization~\cite{aspnes2006localization}, and evaluate it by
simulation with an idealized common offset. Surveys of UAV spoofing
defenses~\cite{altaweel2023fanets,sebastian2026survey} note that
detectors relying on neighbours' positions fail when the neighbours are
inside the spoofed area, but none quantifies the effect for a physical
ground array.

\subsection{Positioning of this work}
Our attacker is deliberately more constrained than those
of~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp,park2026rigidshift}: fixed transmitters on
the ground, no per-victim adaptation, a measured channel. The quantity we
report is precisely what separates it from the ideal formation-preserving
attack---a dilation of the computed formation, modeled and
validated---and its consequence for the relative-distance detectors over
the spacing range of real formation flight, including the two-sided
aggregate test that the dilation calls for.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Swarm, constellation, and measurement model}
We consider a swarm of $m=25$ UAVs flying in a square $5\times5$ grid
with spacing $g$ (the \emph{formation spacing}), following a way-point
trajectory at altitudes between 100 and 700\,m that spans about 7\,km
around its start point; positions are sampled every $T_s=5$\,s over a
20-minute mission. The GNSS constellation is modeled as
$N_{\mathrm{sat}}=24$ satellites on 6 circular orbital planes
(inclination $55^\circ$, altitude 20\,200\,km) over a rotating spherical
Earth. For UAV $u$ at position $\ptrue{u}(t)$ and satellite $s$ at
position $\mathbf{s}_s(t)$ (visible when its elevation is positive), the
authentic propagation delay is
$\tau_{u,s}(t)=\lVert\mathbf{s}_s(t)-\ptrue{u}(t)\rVert/c$. Each receiver
has a clock bias $\delta t_u\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)$\,s, constant over the
mission, and each pseudorange carries additive white Gaussian noise of
standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{pr}}=3$\,m, representative of
standard (non-differential) positioning.

Each UAV computes its position fix $\pest{u}$ independently by iterated
linearized least squares (Gauss--Newton) over the four unknowns
$(x,y,z,\delta t)$: at iteration $k$, with predicted ranges
$R_s=\lVert\mathbf{s}_s-\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\rVert$ and pseudoranges
$\rho_s=c\,(\tilde{\tau}_{u,s}-\widehat{\delta t}^{(k)})$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{G}\,\Delta =\boldsymbol{\rho}-\mathbf{R},\qquad
\mathbf{G}_{s,:}=\Bigl[\tfrac{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{s}_s)^{\!\top}}{R_s},\; c\Bigr],
\label{eq:ls}
\end{equation}
solved in the least-squares sense for five iterations, where
$\tilde{\tau}_{u,s}$ is the \emph{measured} delay (authentic or spoofed).
Without attack and noise the solver returns the true grid positions to
numerical precision (error $<10^{-4}$\,m), so estimated and true
positions are expressed in the same frame.

In addition to GNSS, the UAVs measure their instantaneous mutual
distances with an independent on-board ranging sensor (UWB two-way
ranging, as in~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}):
\begin{equation}
d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t) = \lVert\ptrue{i}(t)-\ptrue{j}(t)\rVert
 + \varepsilon_{ij}(t),\quad
\varepsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{rng}}),
\label{eq:uwb}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{\mathrm{rng}}=0.1$\,m. This is the most favorable setting
for the defender: the true formation is known essentially without error
at every epoch.

\subsection{Ground-to-UAV channel}
Spoofing signals travel over a ground-to-air link. We adopt the
suburban cellular-to-UAV path-loss model of Al-Hourani and
Gomez~\cite{alhourani2018cellular}: for a ground transmitter at
horizontal distance $d$ and elevation angle $\theta$ (in degrees) as seen
from the UAV,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PL}(d,\theta) = 10\,\alpha\log_{10}(d)
 + A\,(\theta-\theta_0)\,e^{-\frac{\theta-\theta_0}{B}} + \eta_0
 \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\label{eq:alhourani}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=3.04$, $A=-23.29$, $B=4.14$, $\theta_0=-3.61^\circ$,
$\eta_0=20.70$\,dB (shadowing disabled for repeatability). The
excess-loss term is fitted in~\cite{alhourani2018cellular} for
$\theta\in[-2^\circ,10^\circ]$ and is saturated at $10^\circ$ beyond;
with the deployment of Section~\ref{sec:attack} the largest angle met in
any run is $6.0^\circ$, so the model is always used within its validity
range. Authentic signals follow free-space loss from satellite transmit
power $P_t^{\mathrm{sat}}=25$\,W with antenna gains
$G_t^{\mathrm{sat}}=13.5$\,dB and $G_r=5$\,dB, giving received powers
of about $-150$\,dBW.

\section{Physical Distributed Spoofing Attack}
\label{sec:attack}

The attacker's goal is to displace the whole swarm's perceived position
onto a decoy point about 5.7~km from the flight path, while the swarm
believes it is following its mission plan. The attack is simulated
transmitter by transmitter; nothing is assumed about per-victim signal
shaping.

\subsection{Transmitter geometry and per-epoch routing}
$N_{\mathrm{sp}}=24$ fixed ground transmitters are placed on a circle of
radius $R_{\mathrm{sp}}=10$\,km enclosing the swarm's operating area
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}), each radiating $P_t^{\mathrm{sp}}=1$\,mW with
$G_t^{\mathrm{sp}}=1$\,dB. Following the geometric argument
of~\cite[Result~7]{tippenhauer2011requirements}, each visible satellite
$s$ is routed, at every epoch, to the transmitter whose azimuth as seen
from the \emph{current} true swarm centre $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}(t)$
is closest to the satellite's azimuth:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sp}(s,t)=\arg\min_{k}\;
\bigl|\angle_{2\pi}\bigl(\phi_s(t)-\phi_k(t)\bigr)\bigr| .
\label{eq:assign}
\end{equation}
The transmitters never move; only the routing is updated, which requires
the attacker to track the swarm centre---the same knowledge it needs to
calibrate the replayed delays.

\subsection{Capture and replayed delay}
For each UAV $u$ and visible satellite $s$ the receiver locks onto the
transmitter whenever the received spoofing power, computed
with~\eqref{eq:alhourani}, exceeds the authentic one. With the chosen
EIRP the spoofer margin over the satellite is between 13 and 29\,dB on
every link (median 19.5\,dB), so capture is complete and homogeneous
across the swarm: no UAV mixes authentic and spoofed satellites.

Transmitter $k=\mathrm{sp}(s,t)$ replays the signal of satellite $s$ with
its code phase calibrated so that, at the true swarm centre, the measured
delay equals the decoy delay $\tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}$---the delay that
satellite $s$ would exhibit at the decoy position. A UAV at a different
position receives the same transmission over a different path and
measures
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\tau}_{u,s} \;=\; \tau^{\mathrm{dec}}_{s}\;-\;\delta t_u\;+\;
\frac{\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\ptrue{u}\rVert-\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}\rVert}{c},
\label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{q}_k$ is the transmitter position and both norms are true
three-dimensional slant distances. The last term is the physical
propagation residual: it vanishes for the centre UAV, which computes
exactly the decoy position, and is nonzero for the other UAVs. Since the
delays in~\eqref{eq:power} do not depend on the transmitted power, the
residual is by construction independent of it once capture is complete.

\subsection{Residual non-rigidity: a ground array dilates the formation}
\label{sec:residual}
Write $\ptrue{u}=\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$ with
$\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}_u\rVert\ll\lVert\mathbf{q}_k-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{ctr}}\rVert$,
and let $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_k$ be the unit vector from the centre to
transmitter $k$ and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s$ the unit line of sight from the
centre to satellite $s$. To first order the delivered residual
in~\eqref{eq:power} is $-(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_k\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)/c$,
whereas the delay that would make UAV $u$ compute the decoy position
\emph{rigidly displaced} by $\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$ is
$-(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)/c$. The
pseudorange error with respect to the rigid attack is therefore
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{u,s}\;=\;(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s-\hat{\mathbf{v}}_k)\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_u
\;\approx\;-(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_s\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_u)\,
\bigl(\cos\theta_k-\cos\mathrm{el}_s\bigr),
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where the approximation holds for a horizontal swarm and azimuth-matched
routing, with $\hat{\mathbf{a}}_s$ the satellite azimuth direction,
$\theta_k$ the near-zero elevation of the ground transmitter and
$\mathrm{el}_s$ the satellite elevation. Since $\cos\theta_k\approx1>
\cos\mathrm{el}_s$, the coefficient is negative for every satellite: a
UAV displaced \emph{towards} the azimuth of satellite $s$ measures a
pseudorange \emph{shorter} than the rigid one, and a UAV displaced away
from it a longer one. The least-squares solver~\eqref{eq:ls} interprets
shorter ranges to the satellites ahead and longer ranges to those behind
as a position farther along $\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$: every UAV is pushed
outward along its own displacement from the centre, and the computed
formation is \emph{dilated}. A ground transmitter cannot reproduce the
satellite's elevation, so~\eqref{eq:residual} vanishes only for
satellites near the horizon.

\emph{Predicted dilation.} Matching~\eqref{eq:residual} with the
linearized position response $-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_s\!\cdot\!\Delta\mathbf{p}$
gives, for a single satellite, $\Delta\mathbf{p}=
(1/\cos\mathrm{el}_s-1)\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$; averaged over the
constellation the dilation is of order
\begin{equation}
E\;\approx\;\frac{1}{\langle\cos\mathrm{el}_s\rangle}-1 ,
\label{eq:dilation}
\end{equation}
which gives 0.3--0.4 for typical elevation distributions and is a useful
upper-bound intuition; the exact first-order prediction is obtained by
solving the same least-squares problem~\eqref{eq:ls} with the error
vector $\{\epsilon_{u,s}\}$ for a unit displacement, which yields a
linear map $\mathbf{M}$ from $\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$ to the position
error (including its vertical component) and hence the relative length
change of every pair of the grid. Figure~\ref{fig:validation} compares
this prediction with the measured distortion, epoch by epoch, on a
noiseless attacked mission at $g=20$\,m: the model reproduces the
measured dilation epoch by epoch through its variations with the
satellite geometry (correlation $1.00$, largest deviation $0.4$
percentage points), with a median predicted value of $24.4$\,\% against
a measured $24.5$\,\%. Two details of the model matter: the vertical
component of the position error, an altitude tilt proportional to
$\boldsymbol{\delta}_u$, accounts for a quarter of the three-dimensional
distortion (the horizontal part alone predicts $18.8$\,\%), and the
clock and vertical degrees of freedom of the solver absorb part of the
pseudorange error, which is why the simple formula~\eqref{eq:dilation}
overestimates ($34.5$\,\%). The variation of the dilation with the
elevations of the satellites in view is also what produces the
mission-to-mission spread of Fig.~\ref{fig:distortion}.

Two further facts matter for an attacker. The residual does not depend
on transmitter power, by construction. It depends critically on the
array enclosing the operating area: with a 2.5\,km array centred on the
start point the swarm is inside the circle only 26\,\% of the time, and
the deformation grows from 12\,\% inside to 55\,\% outside (correlation
$0.98$ with the distance from the array centre), because from outside
the array all transmitters lie in a narrow angular sector and no routing
can match the satellite azimuths.

\begin{remark}[Which tail to test]
\label{rem:collapse}
The collapse detectors of Section~\ref{sec:detection} threshold a
statistic that decreases when the pairwise distances shrink---the
signature of a single transmitter seen by all
receivers~\cite[Result~1]{tippenhauer2011requirements}. A ground array
with one transmitter per satellite dilates the formation instead: the
same statistic \emph{increases}, and the attack sits in the tail these
detectors never examine. The dilation is nevertheless a systematic
bias of the distance ratios, and a two-sided test on an aggregate
statistic is the natural tool to detect it.
\end{remark}

\section{Relative-Distance Detection Methods}
\label{sec:detection}

All detectors operate on the normalized distance ratios
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}(t)=\frac{\hat{d}_{ij}(t)}{d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}(t)},
\qquad
\hat{d}_{ij}=\lVert\pest{i}-\pest{j}\rVert ,
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
computed over all $\binom{m}{2}=300$ pairs at every epoch, where
$d^{\mathrm{meas}}_{ij}$ is the instantaneous UWB measurement
of~\eqref{eq:uwb}; under authentic conditions $r_{ij}\approx 1$.

\subsection{Collapse tests (Jansen et al.)}
The ACSAC~2016 detector~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} declares spoofing
when the pairwise distances \emph{shrink}:
\begin{equation}
f\bigl(\{r_{ij}\}\bigr)\;\underset{H_1}{\overset{H_0}{\gtrless}}\;\lambda,
\qquad \text{alarm if } f<\lambda,
\label{eq:jansen}
\end{equation}
with the three variants of its Appendix~A implemented here:
\begin{align}
f_{\min}  &= \min_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\max}  &= \max_{i<j} r_{ij}, &
f_{\mathrm{maj}} &= \operatorname*{med}_{i<j} r_{ij},
\label{eq:variants}
\end{align}
where the median form is equivalent to the paper's normalized majority
voting: at least half of the pairs below $\lambda$ iff the median is.
Jansen et al.\ set $\lambda$ from a Rician ($H_0$) / Rayleigh ($H_1$)
model of the pairwise distance with spatially correlated position noise,
$\sigma_d=\sqrt{2}\,\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}$; we calibrate $\lambda$
empirically to the same effect (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsection{Two-sided tests}
Two-sided tests fire on any deviation of the distance ratios from one.
The \emph{worst-pair} test
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{wp}} = \max_{i<j}\,\bigl|\,r_{ij}-1\,\bigr|,
\qquad \text{alarm if } T_{\mathrm{wp}}>\lambda ,
\label{eq:cons}
\end{equation}
is the form used by the GNSS-vs-UWB check of~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb}
($|d_{\mathrm{GPS}}-d_{\mathrm{UWB}}|>d_{\mathrm{thr}}$ on any pair) and
by per-measurement double-difference tests~\cite{stenberg2022multiple}
when their outcomes are combined by a single exceedance. Because it is
governed by the noisiest pair, it is sensitive to outliers and weak
against a bias shared by all pairs. Against such a bias an
\emph{aggregate} statistic is more powerful---the $\chi^2$ sum
of~\cite{stenberg2022multiple}, or simply the median---and we therefore
also evaluate the \emph{two-sided majority} test
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{maj}} = \bigl|\operatorname*{med}_{i<j} r_{ij}-1\bigr|,
\qquad \text{alarm if } T_{\mathrm{maj}}>\lambda ,
\label{eq:maj2}
\end{equation}
the two-sided counterpart of $f_{\mathrm{maj}}$ in~\eqref{eq:variants},
which is what Remark~\ref{rem:collapse} calls for. Finally, since the
dilation is persistent, we report the worst-pair test averaged over a
sliding window of 12 epochs (1\,min) as an example of temporal
integration.

\subsection{Threshold calibration and metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Following the Neyman--Pearson practice
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver}, thresholds are calibrated on the
$H_0$ (no-spoofing) scenario simulated with the same noise levels:
$\lambda$ is the empirical $\PFA$-quantile (lower for the collapse tests,
upper for the two-sided tests) of the statistic over all $H_0$ epochs,
with $\PFA=10^{-2}$. Performance is reported as (i) the detection
probability $\PD$, the fraction of attacked epochs that raise an alarm;
(ii) ROC curves and their area (AUC) obtained by sweeping $\lambda$,
oriented so that AUC $=0.5$ is chance and AUC $<0.5$ means that the
attack moves the statistic to the side opposite to the alarm region;
and (iii) the median relative distance distortion
$E=\operatorname{med}_{i<j} |\hat{d}_{ij}-d_{ij}|/d_{ij}$, the geometric
evidence available to any relative-distance detector. Because the epochs
of one mission share clocks, trajectory and satellite geometry, the Monte
Carlo unit is the \emph{mission}: 95\,\% confidence intervals on $\PD$
are obtained by a paired bootstrap over missions (500 resamples; $H_0$
and attack of a mission share the random seed, and the threshold is
re-calibrated in every resample).

\section{Simulation Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup and formation-spacing regime}
\label{sec:setup}
Table~\ref{tab:params} summarizes the parameters. We sweep the formation
spacing over $g\in\{5,8,10,12,15,20\}$\,m. This range is the one of
outdoor formation flight on GPS: experimental leader--follower formations
of quadcopters are flown at 5\,m spacing, with separation errors of
0.4\,m~\cite{cheok2022formation} to 1.4--1.9\,m RMSE even with RTK
corrections~\cite{grundler2025mesh}; a design method that derives the
minimum safe spacing from the combined navigation and control error
yields spacings of metres for GNSS-based
navigation~\cite{guo2025separation}; the multi-receiver prototype
of~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} uses a 5\,m square, and the swarm
detector of~\cite{mykytyn2023uwb} is designed around a 5\,m GPS error
budget. Closer spacings, down to about one metre in drone light
shows,\footnote{Show drones are flown at a minimum in-air spacing of
0.8\,m with RTK positioning (vendor data, 2026).} require
centimetre-level RTK positioning and are outside the standard-GPS regime
considered here, while wider spacings belong to coverage and
surveillance swarms rather than to formation flight. For each spacing we
simulate $N_{\mathrm{MC}}=20$ independent missions (random start time,
clock biases and noise), each of 240 epochs, with and without the attack.
The simulator and detection code are available in the companion
repository.%

\subsection{Attack effectiveness}
The attack succeeds: the centre UAV's estimated position is dragged by
the full decoy offset, 4.0\,km on average over the mission, at every
epoch (Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}, top), and every UAV in the swarm is
captured on all its visible satellites. The bottom panel shows the price
paid by a ground array: the relative-distance distortion is raised above
the no-attack noise floor throughout the mission.

\subsection{Formation distortion and dilation}
Figure~\ref{fig:distortion} reports the median relative distance
distortion $E$ over the 20 missions (band: 10th--90th percentile across
missions). Under $H_0$ the distortion is pure GNSS noise and decays as
$1/g$, from 22\,\% at $g=5$\,m to 4.6\,\% at 20\,m. Under attack it
decreases from 38\,\% at 5\,m to 23\,\% at 20\,m, i.e.\ towards the
scale-free plateau of the residual of Section~\ref{sec:residual}. Its
sign is a dilation: the median distance ratio
$\operatorname{med}\,r_{ij}$ is $1.23$ under attack at $g=20$\,m
against $1.01$ without attack ($1.37$ against $1.16$ at 5\,m, where
noise biases both upward), and it exceeds one in every attacked epoch.
The computed formation is enlarged by about a quarter of the baseline;
it never collapses.

First, \textbf{the collapse detectors test the wrong tail}. All three
Jansen variants give $\PD\le0.011$ (95\,\% upper bound), at or below
the false-alarm rate, at every spacing. Their AUC is \emph{below}
chance---down to $0.001$ for the majority variant at 20\,m---which does
not mean that the attack is inseparable from authentic operation: the
attacked statistic is \emph{larger} than the authentic one in
essentially every epoch, i.e.\ perfectly separable, but on the side
these one-sided tests never examine (Remark~\ref{rem:collapse}). The
majority variant, the one recommended in~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver},
never fires at all; no threshold choice changes this.

Second, \textbf{the worst-pair test is masked by noise}: $\PD$ rises
only from $0.03$ at 5\,m to $0.26$ at 20\,m (AUC $0.57$--$0.74$), with
wide confidence intervals that reflect the mission-to-mission
variability of the dilation. The statistic~\eqref{eq:cons} is governed
by the noisiest of 300 pairs, whose $H_0$ tail at
$\sigma_{\mathrm{pr}}=3$\,m is wider than the dilation of the typical
pair over the whole formation-flight regime; averaging it over a
one-minute window raises $\PD$ to $0.12$--$0.38$, still far from
reliable detection.

Third, \textbf{the two-sided majority test detects the dilation at every
spacing}: $\PD=0.38$ at 5\,m, $0.63$ at 10\,m and $0.98$ at 20\,m, with
AUC from $0.83$ to $0.999$. The aggregate statistic~\eqref{eq:maj2}
averages the noise over all pairs and retains the common bias, which is
exactly the signature left by a ground array. Its ROC at 10\,m
(Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}) is the mirror image of the collapse majority
test's: the same statistic, tested on the right side.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsubsection*{What a ground array can and cannot do}
A distributed ground spoofer defeats the premise of the classical
multi-receiver countermeasure---it does not collapse the formation---but
it cannot preserve it either: the elevation mismatch
of~\eqref{eq:residual} dilates the computed formation by about
$1/\langle\cos\mathrm{el}\rangle-1\approx23\,\%$ of the baseline,
independently of transmitter power and of the formation spacing. The
practical consequence for the defender is a change of signature, not a
loss of observability: collapse tests should be replaced, or at least
complemented, by two-sided aggregate tests on the distance ratios, which
detect the dilation reliably at 15--20\,m and in a majority of epochs at
5--10\,m with standard positioning. Temporal integration helps the
worst-pair test but does not make it competitive with the aggregate
statistic; lower-noise fixes (RTK) or wider formations would raise all
two-sided detectors, since the dilation is scale-free while the noise
floor decays as $1/g$. Raising the transmitters would reduce the
dilation according to~\eqref{eq:residual}; we leave that trade-off, and
the design of a spoofer that compensates it, to future work.

\subsubsection*{Scope of the result}
The evaluation covers detectors that are functions of relative
position/distance quantities, over the spacing of GPS-based formation
flight. It does \emph{not} extend to (i) \emph{direction-of-arrival}
detectors: a circle of ground transmitters produces arrivals at
near-zero elevation, incongruent with the satellite constellation, so
multi-antenna arrays with attitude
information~\cite{psiaki2016gnss,borio2016sumofsquares} would see our
attacker, unless its transmitters are placed along the satellite
directions as in~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp}; nor to (ii)
\emph{absolute-reference} checks---ranging to fixed infrastructure, TDoA
from ground sensor networks~\cite{jansen2018crowdgpssec}, terrain or
signal-of-opportunity positioning---for which the 4\,km displacement is
directly observable; nor to (iii) GNSS/INS detectors such as
SemperFi~\cite{sathaye2022semperfi} and INS innovation
monitors~\cite{tanil2018insmonitor,kujur2024insmonitor}, which respond to
the time variation of the induced error and were not evaluated.

\subsubsection*{Relation to formation-preserving attacks}
Tippenhauer et al.~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements} prove that a
formation-preserving placement exists and
GNSS-WASP~\cite{tibaldo2025gnsswasp} realizes it with transmitters in
line with the satellites at altitude; Park and
Yoo~\cite{park2026rigidshift} show that an exactly common translation is
invisible to any relative-only detector. Our attacker is deliberately
more constrained, and the quantity we measure and model is what
separates it from those ideal attacks: a dilation large enough to be
detected by the right test, and the reason why detectors tuned to the
single-transmitter signature miss it.

\subsubsection*{Deployment, steady state, and other simplifications}
The result assumes an array that encloses the swarm's operating area
(Section~\ref{sec:residual}); a static array that the swarm leaves
deforms the formation by more than 50\,\% and is readily detected. The
decoy position is constant, so the attack is modeled in its steady state
after capture; the transient at capture and time-varying decoys are not
modeled. The simulator uses a spherical Earth, a synthetic
constellation, uncorrelated pseudorange noise (spatial correlation would
lower the detector's noise floor~\cite{jansen2016multireceiver} and
favour the two-sided tests), no multipath or shadowing, and signal
capture by power comparison without tracking-loop dynamics. Twenty
missions per spacing bound $\PD$ to within about $\pm0.1$ for the
two-sided tests; the conclusions on the collapse detectors do not depend
on this resolution.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We simulated a physical distributed ground spoofer---24 fixed
transmitters, one impersonated satellite each, real propagation and
channel---attacking a UAV swarm in the 5--20\,m spacing of outdoor
GPS-based formation flight, and evaluated the classical relative-distance
detectors against it. The attack displaces the swarm by 4\,km but cannot
preserve its formation: the mismatch between satellite and transmitter
elevations dilates the computed formation by about 23\,\% of the
baseline, a value reproduced epoch by epoch by a first-order model. The
dilation lies in the tail that the collapse detectors of Jansen et al.\
never test ($\PD\le0.011$, AUC down to $0.001$) and is largely masked by
GNSS noise for the worst-pair GNSS-vs-UWB check ($\PD\le0.26$), but a
two-sided test on the median distance ratio detects it at every spacing
($\PD$ from $0.38$ to $0.98$). Against distributed ground spoofers,
relative-distance detection should therefore test for dilation rather
than collapse, and be complemented by direction-of-arrival or
absolute-reference information for the attackers that can forge the
elevations too.
